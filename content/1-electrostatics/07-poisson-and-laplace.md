*Lecture 7 · course notes §7 · slides "Boundary Conditions; Poisson's and Laplace's Equation" (second half) · prev: [[1-electrostatics/06-circulation-and-boundary-conditions|Lecture 6]] · next: [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Electrostatics has two field equations, $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$. The first is solved once and for all by $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$; putting that into the second leaves a single **scalar** equation for $V$: **Poisson's equation** $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\epsilon$, or **Laplace's equation** $\nabla^2V = 0$ wherever there is no charge. In one dimension it is solved by integrating twice and using the boundary values to fix two constants — the workhorse of the capacitance calculations to come. Its general solution is the superposition of point-charge potentials you already know, which also proves the Helmholtz theorem promised in Lecture 4.

## 1. From Gauss's law to Poisson's equation

Take $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$ with $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$:

$$
\nabla\cdot(\epsilon\,\nabla V) = -\rho \quad\xrightarrow{\ \epsilon\ \text{constant}\ }\quad \epsilon\,\nabla\cdot\nabla V = -\rho .
$$

> [!key] Poisson's and Laplace's equations (homogeneous medium: $\epsilon$ independent of position)
> $$
> \nabla^2V = -\frac{\rho}{\epsilon},\qquad\qquad \nabla^2V = 0\ \ (\rho = 0),
> $$
> where the **Laplacian** is $\nabla^2V \equiv \nabla\cdot\nabla V = \dfrac{\partial^2V}{\partial x^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2V}{\partial y^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2}$ in Cartesian coordinates (cylindrical and spherical forms on [[0-toolkit/02-vector-calculus-cheatsheet|the cheat sheet]]). Poisson's equation *is* Gauss's law, written for the potential. In vacuum $\epsilon = \epsilon_0$.

Two conditions were used and both matter. "Static" (or quasi-static) is what allows $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ at all. "$\epsilon$ constant" is what allows pulling $\epsilon$ out of the divergence; in a medium whose permittivity varies with position, or across the boundary between two dielectrics, $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\epsilon$ is simply false and you must go back to $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon\nabla V) = -\rho$ ([[1-electrostatics/09-static-fields-in-dielectric-media|Lecture 9]]). In a *piecewise* homogeneous arrangement — two dielectric layers, say — Laplace's equation holds inside each layer, and the layers are stitched together with the boundary conditions of Lecture 6.

> [!intuition] What the Laplacian measures
> $\nabla^2V$ at a point is proportional to (average of $V$ over a small sphere around the point) − (value of $V$ at the point). Laplace's equation therefore says: **in a charge-free region, $V$ at every point equals the average of $V$ around it** — no bumps, no dips. Potentials in empty space have no local maxima or minima; the extremes are always on the boundary (which is why a charge cannot be held in stable equilibrium by static fields alone). Positive charge makes $V$ curve *downward* ($\nabla^2V<0$: dome-like, $V$ above its local average), negative charge makes it curve upward (bowl-like). Only at the charge itself is there an actual peak or pit — the pn junction below has $V''<0$ throughout its positive slab while $V$ simply rises to a plateau.

Two more facts you may use freely: the solution of Poisson's or Laplace's equation with the potential specified on the boundary of a region is **unique**, so any function that satisfies the equation and the boundary values is *the* answer — guessing is a legitimate method. And the equation is **linear**, so solutions superpose.

## 2. Solving Laplace's equation in one dimension

When the geometry has enough symmetry that $V$ depends on a single coordinate, the partial differential equation collapses to an ordinary one that you integrate twice:

| symmetry | Laplace's equation | general solution | used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| planar, $V(z)$ | $\dfrac{d^2V}{dz^2} = 0$ | $V = Az + B$ | parallel plates |
| cylindrical, $V(r)$ | $\dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{d}{dr}\Big(r\dfrac{dV}{dr}\Big) = 0$ | $V = A\ln r + B$ | coaxial cable |
| spherical, $V(r)$ | $\dfrac{1}{r^2}\dfrac{d}{dr}\Big(r^2\dfrac{dV}{dr}\Big) = 0$ | $V = \dfrac{A}{r} + B$ | concentric spheres |

> [!recipe] Laplace between conductors
> 1. **Symmetry:** decide which single coordinate $V$ can depend on. (Infinite plates: only $z$. A long coax: only $r$.)
> 2. **Integrate twice** in that coordinate system (table above) to get $V$ with two constants.
> 3. **Boundary values:** the potential of each conductor fixes the constants (conductors are equipotentials — [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]]).
> 4. $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$, then $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$.
> 5. **Surface charges** on the conductors from $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$, with $\hat{n}$ pointing out of the conductor into the field region (the field inside the conductor is zero).
>
> The chain $V\to\mathbf{E}\to\mathbf{D}\to\rho_s$ is exactly the "given $V$" route to capacitance in [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance|Lecture 10]].

> [!example] Example — parallel plates
> Two infinite conducting plates, $d = 2$ m apart in $z$; the plate at $z=0$ is grounded and the plate at $z=d$ is held at $-3$ V. Nothing depends on $x$ or $y$, so $V = V(z)$ and $\nabla^2V = V''(z) = 0$ between the plates (vacuum, no charge): $V = Az + B$. $V(0) = 0$ gives $B = 0$; $V(2) = -3$ gives $A = -\tfrac32$ V/m.
> $$
> V(z) = -\tfrac32 z\ \text{V},\qquad \mathbf{E} = -\nabla V = -\hat{z}\frac{dV}{dz} = +\tfrac32\,\hat{z}\ \text{V/m}\quad(0<z<2\ \text{m}).
> $$
> The field points from the 0 V plate toward the $-3$ V plate — from high to low potential — and is uniform. In symbols, $V = V_0z/d$ and $\mathbf{E} = -(V_0/d)\hat{z}$ for a top plate at $V_0$.
>
> *Plate charges.* Outside the plates the field is zero (the two plates carry equal and opposite sheets whose fields cancel there). At the bottom plate $\hat{n} = +\hat{z}$ points into the gap: $\rho_s(0) = \hat{z}\cdot\epsilon_0\mathbf{E} = +\tfrac32\epsilon_0 \approx 1.3\times10^{-11}$ C/m². At the top plate $\hat{n} = -\hat{z}$: $\rho_s(d) = -\hat{z}\cdot\epsilon_0\mathbf{E} = -\tfrac32\epsilon_0$ C/m². Equal and opposite, as they must be. Notice what this implies: with $+\rho_s$ below and $-\rho_s$ above, the two sheets' fields cancel *inside each metal plate* — the field inside a conductor is zero, and a conductor is an equipotential. Lecture 8 makes this a principle.

> [!example] Example — reading a potential difference off a surface charge
> Two copper blocks face each other across a 3 m gap in $x$; the facing surfaces carry $\rho_s = +2$ C/m² (left block, at $x=0$, held at $V=0$) and $-2$ C/m² (right block). What is the right block's potential $V_p$?
>
> Inside copper $\mathbf{D} = 0$. At $x=0$ with $\hat{n} = \hat{x}$ pointing out of the left block into the gap, $\hat{x}\cdot(\mathbf{D}_{\text{gap}} - 0) = 2$, so $E_x = 2/\epsilon_0$ V/m, uniform across the gap. The potential drop across the gap is $E_xd = 6/\epsilon_0$ V, and since $\mathbf{E}$ points from high to low potential, the right block is *lower*: $V_p = -6/\epsilon_0 \approx -6.8\times10^{11}$ V. (An absurd number — 2 C/m² is an enormous surface charge; real capacitors carry microcoulombs per square metre. The method is the point: $\mathbf{D}$ jump ⇒ $E$ ⇒ $\lvert\Delta V\rvert = Ed$ ⇒ sign from the direction of $\mathbf{E}$.)

## 3. Poisson's equation where there is charge

Inside a charged region Laplace's equation gives way to Poisson's, and the integrations pick up the charge density. The canonical example is the pn junction of [[1-electrostatics/03-gauss-law-at-work#4-superposition-of-the-building-blocks|Lecture 3]]: a slab of $-\rho_1$ on $-W_1<x<0$ next to a slab of $+\rho_2$ on $0<x<W_2$, with $\rho_1W_1 = \rho_2W_2$ (neutral overall), whose field is

$$
E_x = \begin{cases}-\dfrac{\rho_1(x+W_1)}{\epsilon_0}, & -W_1<x<0\\[8pt] \dfrac{\rho_2(x-W_2)}{\epsilon_0}, & 0<x<W_2\end{cases}
\qquad(\text{zero outside}).
$$

> [!example] Example — the built-in potential of a junction
> $V$ depends on $x$ only, so Poisson's equation reads $V'' = -\rho(x)/\epsilon_0$. Integrating once gives $V' = -E_x$ (of course); integrating again, with the constants chosen so that $V_1 = V(-W_1)$ and $V_2 = V(W_2)$ are the plateau values,
> $$
> V(x) = \begin{cases}\dfrac{\rho_1(x+W_1)^2}{2\epsilon_0} + V_1, & -W_1<x<0\\[8pt] -\dfrac{\rho_2(x-W_2)^2}{2\epsilon_0} + V_2, & 0<x<W_2 .\end{cases}
> $$
> A point in space has one potential, so the two branches must agree at $x=0$:
> $$
> \frac{\rho_1W_1^2}{2\epsilon_0} + V_1 = -\frac{\rho_2W_2^2}{2\epsilon_0} + V_2
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> V_{21}\equiv V_2 - V_1 = \frac{\rho_1W_1^2 + \rho_2W_2^2}{2\epsilon_0} = \frac{\rho_2W_2\,(W_1+W_2)}{2\epsilon_0} .
> $$
> (The last step uses neutrality, $\rho_1W_1 = \rho_2W_2$.) The positively charged side sits at the higher potential, consistent with $\mathbf{E}$ pointing in $-\hat{x}$. Choosing $V(0) = 0$ makes the plateaus $V_1 = -\rho_1W_1^2/2\epsilon_0$ and $V_2 = +\rho_2W_2^2/2\epsilon_0$. The curvature tells the story: $V'' = +\rho_1/\epsilon_0>0$ in the negative slab (bowl) and $V'' = -\rho_2/\epsilon_0<0$ in the positive slab (dome). Solved for the widths — $W_1+W_2 = \sqrt{2\epsilon_0V_{21}\,(\rho_1+\rho_2)/(\rho_1\rho_2)}$ — this is the depletion-width formula of ECE 440, and [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance#7-a-nonlinear-capacitor-the-diode-junction|Lecture 10]] turns it into a capacitance.

> [!trap] Where Laplace's equation may and may not be used
> - Only where $\rho = 0$ *inside* the region. Charge on the *bounding* conductors is fine — it enters through the boundary values — but a charged sheet in the middle of the gap splits the problem into two Laplace regions joined by a $\mathbf{D}$-jump ([[1-electrostatics/09-static-fields-in-dielectric-media|Lecture 9]]).
> - Only where $\epsilon$ is constant. Two layers ⇒ two Laplace problems plus matching.
> - The exam's inverse use is just as common: given $\mathbf{E}$ or $V$, the charge is $\rho = \epsilon\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = -\epsilon\nabla^2V$. Zero curl does not mean zero charge.
> - A Lecture 10 slide writes the plate solution as "$V(z) = V_0x/d$": read $V(x) = V_0x/d$ (or $V(z) = V_0z/d$, depending on which axis crosses the gap). A symbol that does not match the coordinate the potential depends on is a sign something slipped (more on the [[0-toolkit/04-errata-in-the-course-materials|errata page]]).

## 4. The general solution: superposition of point charges

For a charge distribution of finite extent with $V\to0$ at infinity, the answer to Poisson's equation is one you already know from Lecture 5:

> [!key] General solution of Poisson's equation
> $$
> V(\mathbf{r}) = \int\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')}{4\pi\epsilon_0\,\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert}\,d^3\mathbf{r}',\qquad d^3\mathbf{r}' = dx'\,dy'\,dz',
> $$
> integrated over the region where $\rho\neq0$. $\mathbf{r}$ is the field point, $\mathbf{r}'$ the source point.

Why this is the solution, in the language of ECE 210: Poisson's equation is a **linear, shift-invariant** system with input $\rho$ and output $V$. Its **impulse response** — the output for $\rho = \delta(\mathbf{r})$, a unit point charge at the origin — is $1/(4\pi\epsilon_0\lvert\mathbf{r}\rvert)$, the point-charge potential. Shift-invariance moves the impulse to $\mathbf{r}'$ and the response to $1/(4\pi\epsilon_0\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert)$ (in EM this is called the **Green's function**). Any $\rho$ is a superposition of impulses, $\rho(\mathbf{r}) = \int\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}')\,d^3\mathbf{r}'$ (the sifting property), so by linearity the response is the same superposition of shifted impulse responses — the formula above. For infinite distributions (sheets, lines, slabs) the integral diverges and one must renormalize by choosing a finite reference, which is what the "$V(0)=0$" instruction in such problems does for you.

## 5. Helmholtz's theorem, finally proved

Lecture 4 claimed that a vector field vanishing at infinity is determined by its divergence and curl. The general solution above is what makes it true. Any such field can be split as

$$
\mathbf{F} = \underbrace{-\nabla V}_{\text{curl-free}} + \underbrace{\nabla\times\mathbf{A}}_{\text{divergence-free}},
$$

and taking the divergence and curl of both sides gives $\nabla^2V = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}$ and (with the gauge choice $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A} = 0$) $\nabla^2\mathbf{A} = -\nabla\times\mathbf{F}$ — two Poisson equations, solved by the integral formula with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf{F}$ playing the role of $\rho/\epsilon_0$:

$$
V(\mathbf{r}) = \int\frac{\nabla'\cdot\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}')}{4\pi\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert}d^3\mathbf{r}',\qquad
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}) = \int\frac{\nabla'\times\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}')}{4\pi\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert}d^3\mathbf{r}' .
$$

So divergence and curl really are the complete "sources" of a field. For a static $\mathbf{E}$ the curl part is absent and $V$ is the Coulomb integral; for the magnetic field of Unit 2 the divergence part is absent and $\mathbf{A}$ — the vector potential — carries everything. ($V$ is fixed up to a constant and $\mathbf{A}$ up to a gradient; this **gauge freedom** never changes $\mathbf{F}$.)

## 6. Summary

- $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\epsilon$ (Poisson), $\nabla^2V = 0$ (Laplace): Gauss's law for the potential, valid where $\epsilon$ is constant.
- 1-D solutions: linear (planar), logarithmic (cylindrical), $1/r$ (spherical); two constants from two boundary values.
- Chain: $V\to\mathbf{E}=-\nabla V\to\mathbf{D}\to\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$ on conductors.
- With charge present: integrate $V'' = -\rho/\epsilon_0$ twice, match $V$ at interfaces (pn junction: $V_{21} = \rho_2W_2(W_1+W_2)/2\epsilon_0$).
- General solution = Coulomb superposition; Helmholtz follows.
