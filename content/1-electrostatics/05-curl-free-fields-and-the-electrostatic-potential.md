*Lecture 5 · course notes §5 · slides "Potential Functions for Static Fields" · prev: [[1-electrostatics/04-divergence-and-curl|Lecture 4]] · next: [[1-electrostatics/06-circulation-and-boundary-conditions|Lecture 6]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Lecture 4 ended with $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$. That one fact buys a whole new tool: a static electric field can be written as the **gradient of a scalar**, $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$, and the scalar $V$ is the familiar **voltage** — the work you must do, per unit charge, to move a test charge from one point to another. Because the field is curl-free, that work does not depend on the route, so $V$ is a well-defined function of position. From $V$ to $\mathbf{E}$ is a derivative; from $\mathbf{E}$ to $V$ is a line integral along any convenient path; from charge to $V$ is scalar superposition, with no vectors to add. Much of the rest of electrostatics — Poisson's equation, conductors as equipotentials, capacitance — is easier in terms of $V$ than in terms of $\mathbf{E}$.

## 1. Why a potential: energy per unit charge

Push a charge $q$ through a displacement $d\mathbf{l}$ in a field $\mathbf{E}$. The field does work $q\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ on the charge; *you* (holding the charge against the field) do the opposite, $-q\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$, and that is the increase in the charge's potential energy:

$$
dU = -q\,\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}.
$$

The energy depends on $q$, which is a nuisance — we want a quantity that describes the *field alone*, the way $gh$ describes a gravitational field regardless of the mass being lifted. Divide by $q$:

> [!key] Potential difference
> $$
> V(b) - V(a) = -\int_a^b \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\qquad[\text{V} = \text{J/C} = \text{N·m/C}]
> $$
> is the work per unit charge needed to carry a test charge from $a$ to $b$ against the field. Equivalently, $\displaystyle\int_a^b\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = V(a) - V(b)$ is the **voltage drop** from $a$ to $b$: the work per unit charge the *field* does on a charge that moves from $a$ to $b$. Units of $\mathbf{E}$: N/C = V/m.

Where does the field's work go? Into kinetic energy if the charge is free to accelerate (a test charge between capacitor plates), or into heat if the charge is dragged through a resistor — either way the energy stored in the field pays for it. When charge is pushed *against* the field (inside a battery) the work done by the field is negative: a voltage *rise* along the direction of motion, and the field's stored energy grows.

## 2. From V to E: the gradient

Write the potential difference for an infinitesimal step, $dV = -\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -(E_x\,dx + E_y\,dy + E_z\,dz)$, and compare it with the total differential of any function of position, $dV = \dfrac{\partial V}{\partial x}dx + \dfrac{\partial V}{\partial y}dy + \dfrac{\partial V}{\partial z}dz$. Matching coefficients:

> [!key] The most important definition of the day
> $$
> \mathbf{E} = -\nabla V,\qquad \nabla V \equiv \hat{x}\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} + \hat{y}\frac{\partial V}{\partial y} + \hat{z}\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}.
> $$
> $\nabla V$ (the **gradient**) points in the direction in which $V$ increases fastest, with magnitude equal to that rate of increase. The minus sign is a convention: it makes $\mathbf{E}$ point from **high** potential to **low** potential, so that positive charges "fall" downhill, the way rocks do.

> [!intuition] Mount Electron
> Think of $V$ as altitude on a topographic map. The contour lines are **equipotentials** — surfaces on which no work is needed to move a charge. The gradient points straight uphill, perpendicular to the contours; $\mathbf{E}$ points straight *downhill*. Where contours crowd together the mountain is steep and the field is strong; where they spread out, the field is weak. Two consequences worth memorizing: **$\mathbf{E}$ is everywhere perpendicular to the equipotentials**, and **$\lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert$ is the potential drop per unit distance** along the steepest direction.

> [!example] Example — E from V
> $V = x^2 - 6y$ V. Then $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V = -2x\,\hat{x} + 6\,\hat{y}$ V/m. At the origin the field is $6\hat{y}$: $V$ decreases with $y$ (the $-6y$ term), and the field points toward decreasing $V$. The equipotentials $x^2 - 6y = \text{const}$ are parabolas, and the arrows in the figure above cross them at right angles.

In cylindrical and spherical coordinates the gradient picks up the usual metric factors (full tables on [[0-toolkit/02-vector-calculus-cheatsheet|the cheat sheet]]):

$$
\nabla V = \hat{r}\frac{\partial V}{\partial r} + \hat{\phi}\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi} + \hat{z}\frac{\partial V}{\partial z},
\qquad
\nabla V = \hat{r}\frac{\partial V}{\partial r} + \hat{\theta}\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta} + \hat{\phi}\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi}.
$$

## 3. Why V exists at all: exact differentials

Any $V$ you write down gives a field $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ that is automatically curl-free, because

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla V) =
\begin{vmatrix}\hat{x}&\hat{y}&\hat{z}\\[2pt] \partial_x&\partial_y&\partial_z\\[2pt] \partial_xV&\partial_yV&\partial_zV\end{vmatrix}
= \hat{x}\,(\partial_y\partial_zV - \partial_z\partial_yV) + \cdots = 0
$$

(mixed partials commute). The converse is the useful direction: **if $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$, a potential exists.** The reason is that $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = E_x\,dx + E_y\,dy + E_z\,dz$ is then an **exact differential** — the total differential of a single function, $-dV$ — so it can be "antidifferentiated". Two tiny fields make the point:

- $\mathbf{E} = y\,\hat{x} + x\,\hat{y}$: $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = y\,dx + x\,dy = d(xy)$, exact. So $dV = -d(xy)$ and $V = -xy + C$. (Check: $-\nabla(-xy) = y\hat{x}+x\hat{y}$ ✓.)
- $\mathbf{E} = y\,\hat{x} - x\,\hat{y}$: $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = y\,dx - x\,dy$ is *not* the differential of anything. Try: $\partial V/\partial x = -y$ forces $V = -xy + f(y,z)$, whence $\partial V/\partial y = -x + \partial f/\partial y$, which can never equal the required $+x$. No $V$ exists — and indeed $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -2\hat{z}\neq 0$.

The constant $C$ is the freedom to put $V=0$ wherever you like: the **ground** or **reference point**. Only *differences* of potential are physical.

> [!trap] $\nabla\cdot\nabla V$ is not zero
> A slide summary says that $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ "satisfies Gauss's law by the identity $\nabla\cdot\nabla V = 0$". Read it as $\nabla\times\nabla V = 0$: that is the identity, and it makes $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ satisfy the *curl* equation automatically. $\nabla\cdot\nabla V = \nabla^2V$ is the Laplacian, and Gauss's law turns it into Poisson's equation, $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\epsilon_0$ ([[1-electrostatics/07-poisson-and-laplace|Lecture 7]]). Curl and divergence answer different questions. (All such slide slips are collected on the [[0-toolkit/04-errata-in-the-course-materials|errata page]].)

## 4. Path independence

The line integral $\int_a^b\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is defined as a limit of sums $\sum_j\mathbf{E}_j\cdot\Delta\mathbf{l}_j$ along a chosen path, with $\Delta\mathbf{l}_j$ pointing from $a$ toward $b$. In general the answer depends on the path. For a curl-free field it cannot, because $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -dV$, and adding up the $-dV$'s along any route from $a$ to $b$ gives $V(a) - V(b)$ whatever the route — the fundamental theorem of calculus in disguise (the gradient theorem of [[1-electrostatics/04-divergence-and-curl#1-from-the-fundamental-theorem-of-calculus-to-stokes|Lecture 4 §1]]).

> [!example] Example — two paths, two fields

> $$
> C_u:\ \int_{y=0}^{1}(\pm x)\,dy\Big|_{x=0} + \int_{x=0}^{1}y\,dx\Big|_{y=1} = 0 + 1 = 1,
> \qquad

> With the $+$ sign both paths give 1: path independent, $\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=0$, and $V(p) - V(o) = -1$ — which is what $V=-xy$ predicts. With the $-$ sign the answers differ ($+1$ versus $-1$), and no potential exists. Their difference, 2, is exactly the flux of $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -2\hat{z}$ through the square with the loop's (clockwise, $-\hat{z}$) orientation: Stokes' theorem, checked.

> [!intuition] What a voltmeter measures
> An ideal voltmeter with its leads on $p$ and $o$ reads $\int_p^o\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ *along its own leads*. It gives a meaningful "voltage between two points" only because the integral is independent of how the leads are routed — which is true for static fields. When Faraday's law appears (Lecture 14, [[2-magnetostatics/index|Unit 2]]) the induced $\mathbf{E}$ has curl, and the reading of a voltmeter genuinely depends on where you drape the wires.

## 5. From E to V: two recipes

Given a field, the potential is $V(\mathbf{r}) = V(\mathbf{r}_{\text{ref}}) - \int_{\mathbf{r}_{\text{ref}}}^{\mathbf{r}}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$. Two ways to evaluate it, both worth owning.

> [!recipe] V from E
> 0. **Check the curl.** If $\nabla\times\mathbf{E}\neq 0$, stop: there is no $V$.
> 1. **Spot the exact differential.** Write $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ and regroup it, using the product rule backwards ($y\,dx + x\,dy = d(xy)$, $y\,dz + z\,dy = d(yz)$, $2x\,dx = d(x^2)$, …), until it reads $d(\text{something})$. Then $V = -(\text{something}) + C$.
> 2. **Or integrate along an axis-parallel staircase** from the reference point: $(0,0,0)\to(x,0,0)\to(x,y,0)\to(x,y,z)$. On each leg only one differential is nonzero, and the other coordinates take their *current* values (0 on the first leg, $x$ fixed on the second, $x$ and $y$ fixed on the third).
> 3. **Fix $C$** from the reference condition ($V=0$ at the origin, or at infinity).
> 4. **Check:** $-\nabla V$ must give back $\mathbf{E}$.

> [!example] Example — both recipes on one field
> $\mathbf{E} = 2x\,\hat{x} + 3z\,\hat{y} + 3(y+1)\,\hat{z}$ V/m with $V(0,0,0) = 0$. (Curl: $\partial_yE_z - \partial_zE_y = 3 - 3 = 0$, the other components trivially zero ✓.)
>
> *Exact differential:* $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 2x\,dx + 3z\,dy + 3y\,dz + 3\,dz = d(x^2) + 3\,d(yz) + 3\,dz = d(x^2 + 3yz + 3z)$, so $V = -x^2 - 3yz - 3z + C$, and $V(0)=0$ gives $C=0$.
>
> *Staircase:* $V(X,Y,Z) = -\int_0^X 2x\,dx\big|_{y=z=0} - \int_0^Y 3z\,dy\big|_{x=X,\,z=0} - \int_0^Z 3(y+1)\,dz\big|_{x=X,\,y=Y} = -X^2 - 0 - 3(Y+1)Z.$
>
> Both give $V(x,y,z) = -x^2 - 3(y+1)z$ V. Check: $-\nabla V = 2x\,\hat{x} + 3z\,\hat{y} + 3(y+1)\,\hat{z}$ ✓. A second one from the slides: $\mathbf{E} = yz\,\hat{x} + (y+zx)\,\hat{y} + xy\,\hat{z}$ gives $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = d(xyz) + y\,dy$, so $V = -xyz - \tfrac12y^2$.

> [!trap] Three ways to lose the sign
> 1. **Forgetting the minus** in $V(b) - V(a) = -\int_a^b\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$. A positive charge sits at high $V$; moving away from it, $V$ must fall.
> 2. **Counting the direction twice.** On a radial path from $\infty$ inward, write $d\mathbf{l} = \hat{r}\,dr$ and let the limits $\int_\infty^{r}$ carry the direction ($dr<0$ along the way, automatically). Writing $d\mathbf{l} = -\hat{r}\,dr$ *and* integrating from $\infty$ to $r$ flips the sign twice.
> 3. **Antidifferentiating each component separately and adding** ($\int E_x\,dx + \int E_y\,dy + \int E_z\,dz$ with all variables free) double-counts the cross terms — in the example above it would count $3yz$ twice. One integral, one path.

## 6. From charge to V: the point charge and superposition

For a point charge $Q$ at the origin, $\mathbf{E} = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}\hat{r}$. Take the reference at infinity and integrate inward along a radius, $d\mathbf{l} = \hat{r}\,dr$:

$$
V(r) = V(\infty) - \int_\infty^{r}\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r'^2}\,dr' = 0 - \Big[-\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r'}\Big]_\infty^{r}
$$

> [!key] Potential of a point charge, and superposition
> $$
> V(r) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0\,r}\quad[\text{V}],\qquad V(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_i\frac{Q_i}{\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i\rvert},\qquad V(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,d^3\mathbf{r}'}{\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert}.
> $$
> $V$ falls as $1/r$ (one power slower than $\mathbf{E}$), its equipotentials are concentric spheres, and — the practical point — superposition of potentials is a **scalar** sum. No components, no unit vectors: add the numbers, then take $-\nabla$ once at the end if you need the field. (The exact-differential route gives the same result in one line: $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \dfrac{Q\,dr}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2} = d\!\Big(\!-\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}\Big) = -dV$.)

> [!example] Example — the hydrogen atom in volts
> The proton's potential at the Bohr radius $a = 0.529\times10^{-10}$ m is $V(a) = \dfrac{e}{4\pi\epsilon_0a} = \dfrac{(1.6\times10^{-19})(9\times10^{9})}{0.529\times10^{-10}} \approx 27.2$ V. The potential energy of a charge $q$ placed at $\mathbf{r}$ is $U = qV(\mathbf{r})$, so an electron there has $U = -e\,V(a) = -27.2$ eV (a second proton would have $+27.2$ eV). The electron-volt, $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J, is just "charge $e$ times a potential of one volt". The hydrogen ground-state energy, $-13.6$ eV, is half of this potential energy — the other half is cancelled by the kinetic energy of the orbiting electron.

> [!trap] When "zero at infinity" is not available
> $V(\infty)=0$ works when the total charge is finite. For an infinite line or sheet or slab the integral $\int_\infty^r\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ diverges, so the problem must hand you a reference point — "$V(0)=0$" in [[problems/charged-slab-with-a-power-law-profile|the slab problem]]. For a line charge, $V(r) = -\dfrac{\rho_l}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln\dfrac{r}{r_0}$ with $V(r_0)=0$: logarithms, not $1/r$. The physics is unchanged; only the bookkeeping constant moves.

## 7. Summary

- $V(b) - V(a) = -\int_a^b\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$: work per unit charge, path-independent because $\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=0$.
- $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$: $\mathbf{E}$ points downhill, perpendicular to the equipotentials, with magnitude "volts per metre".
- Existence of $V$ ⇔ $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ exact ⇔ curl-free ⇔ conservative ([[concepts/conservative-field]]).
- $V$ from $\mathbf{E}$: check curl → exact differential or staircase path → fix the constant → verify with $-\nabla V$.
- $V$ from charge: $Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)$ and scalar superposition; $U = qV$.
- The reference point is yours to choose; only differences matter.
