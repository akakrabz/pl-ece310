*Lecture 10 · course notes §10 · slides "Capacitance and Conductance" · prev: [[1-electrostatics/09-static-fields-in-dielectric-media|Lecture 9]] · next: Lecture 11 (Lorentz–Drude models for σ and χe; not on Exam 1)*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Put charge $+Q$ on one conductor and $-Q$ on another and a potential difference $V$ appears between them. Because electrostatics is linear, doubling $Q$ doubles $V$: the ratio $C = Q/V$ is a constant of the *geometry and the dielectric* — the **capacitance**, in farads. You never look it up; you compute it by running the chain of the last five lectures in whichever direction the problem allows: from a given $V$ through Laplace's equation to the charge, or from a given $Q$ through Gauss's law to the voltage. Three geometries cover the course. Fill the gap with a slightly conducting material and the same field pattern also carries a leakage current $I = GV$, with $G = (\sigma/\epsilon)C$; the stored energy is $\tfrac12CV^2$, spread through the field at $\tfrac12\epsilon E^2$ per cubic metre; and a diode's depletion layer is a capacitor whose $C$ shrinks as the voltage grows.

## 1. Definition, and the two recipes

> [!key] Capacitance
> For two conductors carrying $+Q$ and $-Q$, with potential difference $V = V_+ - V_-> 0$ between them,
> $$
> Q = CV,\qquad C = \frac{Q}{V}\quad[\text{F} = \text{C/V}].
> $$
> $C$ depends only on the shapes, the separation, and $\epsilon$ of the filling — not on $Q$ or $V$ (for linear media). $V$ here is the **drop** from the positive conductor to the negative one, so $C>0$ always.

> [!recipe] Two routes to C
> **Given $V$** (the conductors' potentials): solve Laplace's equation for $V(\mathbf{r})$ with those boundary values → $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ → $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ → $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$ on **one** conductor ($\hat{n}$ out of the conductor) → $Q = \int\rho_s\,dA$ → $C = Q/V$.
>
> **Given $Q$** (the charge on the conductors): Gauss's law (or sheet superposition) → $\mathbf{D}$ → $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon$ → $V = -\int_-^+\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ from the negative conductor to the positive one (a positive number) → $C = Q/V$.
>
> Either way the chain passes through the same field pattern, and $C$ comes out the same. Pick the route that matches what you are given; on an exam, the given-$Q$ route is usually shorter.

Two conditions keep the idealization honest. **No fringing:** the field pattern is that of infinite plates (or an infinitely long coax) only if the gap is small compared with the plate size, $d\ll\sqrt{A}$; otherwise field lines bulge out at the edges and $C$ is somewhat larger. **Quasi-static:** $Q=CV$ describes a time-varying capacitor only if the structure is small compared with a wavelength, $\sqrt{A}\ll c/f$; a large structure driven fast is a transmission line, not a lumped element ([[4-transmission-lines/index|Unit 4]]).

## 2. Parallel plates, both ways

Plates of area $A$ at $x=0$ and $x=d$; plate at $x=d$ held at $V_0$, plate at $x=0$ grounded; dielectric $\epsilon$ between.

**Given $V$.** $V(x) = V_0x/d$ (Laplace: linear, matching both plates), so $\mathbf{E} = -\dfrac{V_0}{d}\hat{x}$ and $\mathbf{D} = -\dfrac{\epsilon V_0}{d}\hat{x}$. At the positive plate ($x=d$) the normal into the gap is $-\hat{x}$: $\rho_s = (-\hat{x})\cdot\mathbf{D} = +\dfrac{\epsilon V_0}{d}$ [C/m²], hence $Q = \rho_sA = \dfrac{\epsilon AV_0}{d}$ and

> [!key] Parallel-plate capacitor
> $$
> C = \frac{\epsilon A}{d}\qquad(d\ll\sqrt{A}).
> $$

**Given $Q$.** The plates carry $\pm Q/A$ on their facing surfaces (nowhere else: the field must vanish inside each conductor and outside the pair). Between them $\mathbf{D} = -\dfrac{Q}{A}\hat{x}$ (pointing from $+$ to $-$), $\mathbf{E} = -\dfrac{Q}{\epsilon A}\hat{x}$, and the drop from the positive plate to the negative one is $V = V(d) - V(0) = -\int_0^dE_x\,dx = \dfrac{Qd}{\epsilon A}$. Then $C = Q/V = \epsilon A/d$ again.

> [!trap] Signs in the given-$Q$ route
> A slide writes the plate voltage as $V = \int_0^d\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$; with $\mathbf{E} = -(Q/\epsilon A)\hat{x}$ this gives a *negative* number. The correct statement is $V(d)-V(0) = -\int_0^d\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$, or "the drop from the positive plate to the negative plate is $\int_+^-\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = Ed$". Decide which conductor is positive, integrate from it to the other, and the drop comes out positive. The same slip produces negative capacitances in coax problems (FA26 4(e) has one in the key's intermediate line; see the [[0-toolkit/04-errata-in-the-course-materials|errata page]]).

**Given $V$** ($V(a) = V_0$, $V(b) = 0$). Laplace in cylindrical coordinates with $V=V(r)$: $\dfrac1r\dfrac{d}{dr}\Big(r\dfrac{dV}{dr}\Big) = 0 \Rightarrow r\dfrac{dV}{dr} = A \Rightarrow V = A\ln r + B$. The boundary values give

$$
V(r) = V_0\,\frac{\ln(b/r)}{\ln(b/a)},\qquad
\mathbf{E} = -\frac{dV}{dr}\hat{r} = \frac{V_0}{\ln(b/a)}\,\frac{1}{r}\,\hat{r},\qquad
\mathbf{D} = \frac{\epsilon V_0}{\ln(b/a)}\,\frac{1}{r}\,\hat{r}.
$$

On the inner conductor ($\hat{n} = \hat{r}$ into the dielectric): $\rho_s(a) = \dfrac{\epsilon V_0}{a\ln(b/a)}$, so $Q = \rho_s\cdot2\pi aL = \dfrac{2\pi\epsilon LV_0}{\ln(b/a)}$. (On the outer conductor, $\rho_s(b) = -\hat{r}\cdot\mathbf{D}(b) = -\dfrac{\epsilon V_0}{b\ln(b/a)}$, and $2\pi bL$ times that is $-Q$ ✓.)

**Given $Q$** (or the charge per unit length $\lambda = Q/L$ — the exam's symbol for what this site otherwise calls $\rho_l$). Gauss on a coaxial cylinder of radius $a<r<b$: $D_r\,2\pi rL = Q$, so $\mathbf{D} = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi r}\hat{r}$, $\mathbf{E} = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon r}\hat{r}$, and the drop from inner to outer is $V = \displaystyle\int_a^b\frac{\lambda\,dr}{2\pi\epsilon r} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon}\ln\frac ba$.

> [!key] Coaxial capacitor
> $$
> C = \frac{2\pi\epsilon L}{\ln(b/a)},\qquad \mathcal{C} \equiv \frac{C}{L} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon}{\ln(b/a)}\quad[\text{F/m}].
> $$
> The **per-unit-length** capacitance $\mathcal{C}$ (script C) is the natural quantity for a cable, and the one transmission-line theory uses. It depends on the radii only through their ratio.

> [!example] Example — two concept checks that train the chain
> A coax filled with $\epsilon = 3\epsilon_0$ has its dielectric replaced by air.
> - *Keeping the same charge on each conductor:* $Q$ same → $\rho_s$ same → $\mathbf{D}$ same → $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon$ **triples** → $V = \int E\,dr$ **triples** → $C = Q/V$ drops by 3. So "the voltage is reduced by 3×" is the **false** statement.
> - *Keeping the same voltage:* $V$ same → $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ **same** → $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ drops by 3 → $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$ and $Q$ drop by 3 → $C$ drops by 3. So "the E-field is reduced by 3×" is the **false** statement.
>
> In both cases $C$ falls by the factor $\epsilon_r$ — it is a property of the structure — but *which* of $Q$ and $V$ moves depends on what is held fixed.

## 4. Layered dielectrics: series and parallel

> [!key] Concentric spheres, and combinations
> For conducting spheres of radii $a<b$ with $\epsilon$ between: Gauss gives $E_r = Q/(4\pi\epsilon r^2)$, the drop is $V = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon}\Big(\dfrac1a - \dfrac1b\Big)$, and
> $$
> C = 4\pi\epsilon\,\frac{ab}{b-a}\ \xrightarrow{\ b\to\infty\ }\ 4\pi\epsilon a\quad(\text{an isolated sphere}).
> $$
> When the field crosses layers **in succession** (layers normal to $\mathbf{E}$), $\mathbf{D}$ is common and the drops add: $\dfrac1C = \sum_i\dfrac1{C_i}$ — **series**. When layers sit **side by side** along the field (interfaces parallel to $\mathbf{E}$), $\mathbf{E}$ is common and the charges add: $C = \sum_iC_i$ — **parallel**.

For plates with layers $d_1$ ($\epsilon_1$) and $d_2$ ($\epsilon_2$): $\dfrac1C = \dfrac{d_1}{\epsilon_1A} + \dfrac{d_2}{\epsilon_2A}$. Check against the two-layer example of [[1-electrostatics/09-static-fields-in-dielectric-media#3-laplaces-equation-layer-by-layer|Lecture 9]]: the capacitance per unit area is $\big(1/\epsilon_0 + 1/(2\epsilon_0)\big)^{-1} = \tfrac23\epsilon_0$ F/m², and with $2\epsilon_0$ C/m² on the plates, $V = (2\epsilon_0)/(\tfrac23\epsilon_0) = 3$ V ✓. For a coax with $\epsilon_1$ on $a<r<c$ and $\epsilon_2$ on $c<r<b$, the same logic gives $\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{C}} = \dfrac{\ln(c/a)}{2\pi\epsilon_1} + \dfrac{\ln(b/c)}{2\pi\epsilon_2}$ — Exam 1 problem 4(e), worked in [[problems/two-layer-coaxial-capacitor]]. If instead two dielectrics each fill *half the angle* of the annulus, $\mathbf{E}$ is the same in both halves and they add in parallel: $\mathcal{C} = \pi(\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2)/\ln(b/a)$.

## 5. Energy stored in a capacitor and in the field

Charge a capacitor gradually. When it holds charge $q$ its voltage is $q/C$, and moving the next $dq$ across costs $dW = (q/C)\,dq$:

> [!key] Stored energy
> $$
> W = \int_0^Q\frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \tfrac12CV^2 = \tfrac12QV\quad[\text{J}].
> $$
> For the parallel plate, $C = \epsilon A/d$ and $V = Ed$ give $W = \tfrac12\epsilon E^2\,(Ad)$: the energy is the volume of the gap times
> $$
> w = \tfrac12\epsilon E^2 = \tfrac12\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{E}\quad[\text{J/m}^3],
> $$
> which is taken to be the **energy density of the electrostatic field** in general. Energy lives in the field, wherever the field is. (A slide writes W/m³ — that is a *power* density. Energy is joules.)

The same result comes from power: $P = VI = V\,C\,dV/dt = \dfrac{d}{dt}\big(\tfrac12CV^2\big)$. Note that a battery at fixed voltage $V$ delivers $QV = CV^2$ while charging the capacitor to $Q$; only half is stored — the other half is dissipated in whatever resistance the charging current flows through, however small. For a coax, $W' = \tfrac12\mathcal{C}V^2$ per metre.

Now let the filling have a small conductivity $\sigma$ as well as its permittivity $\epsilon$. The same $\mathbf{E}$ that stores the charge drives a current density $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$ from the positive conductor to the negative one. For the parallel plate, $J = \sigma V/d$ and $I = JA = \dfrac{\sigma A}{d}V$:

> [!key] Conductance, and its relation to capacitance
> $$
> I = GV,\qquad G = \frac{\sigma A}{d}\ (\text{plates}),\qquad R = \frac1G = \frac{d}{\sigma A},\qquad\boxed{\ G = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon}\,C\ }.
> $$
> $G$ [S] and $C$ [F] share one **geometric factor** ($A/d$ for plates, $2\pi L/\ln(b/a)$ for a coax, $4\pi ab/(b-a)$ for spheres): $C = \epsilon\times$(factor) and $G = \sigma\times$(factor). So $G = (\sigma/\epsilon)C$ for *any* geometry whose field region is filled with a single homogeneous medium — it fails for layered fillings unless every layer has the same $\sigma/\epsilon$.

> [!recipe] Conductance directly
> $V\to\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V\to\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}\to I = \int\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ through any surface separating the conductors $\to G = I/V$ (and $\mathcal{G} = G/L$ per unit length).
>
> *Coax:* $\mathbf{J} = \dfrac{\sigma V_0}{\ln(b/a)}\dfrac1r\hat{r}$; through a cylinder of radius $r$, $I = J_r\cdot2\pi rL = \dfrac{2\pi\sigma LV_0}{\ln(b/a)}$ — the $1/r$ cancels the $r$ in the area, so the current is the same through every such cylinder (that is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$, steady current). Hence $G = \dfrac{2\pi\sigma L}{\ln(b/a)}$ and $\mathcal{G} = \dfrac{2\pi\sigma}{\ln(b/a)}$ [S/m]. *Spheres:* shells in series, $dR = \dfrac{dr}{4\pi\sigma r^2}$, so $R = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\sigma}\Big(\dfrac1a - \dfrac1b\Big)$ and $G = 4\pi\sigma\dfrac{ab}{b-a} = \dfrac{\sigma}{\epsilon}C$ ✓.

**The circuit model.** Charge on the positive conductor changes at the rate at which external current brings it in, minus the rate at which leakage carries it across: $\dfrac{dQ}{dt} = C\dfrac{dV}{dt} = I - GV$, i.e.

$$
I = C\frac{dV}{dt} + GV:
$$

a capacitance $C$ in **parallel** with a resistance $R = 1/G$. With no external circuit ($I=0$) the charge leaks away by itself: $C\,dV/dt = -GV$, so

> [!key] Self-discharge and the relaxation time
> $$
> Q(t) = Q(0)\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau = RC = \frac{C}{G} = \frac{\epsilon}{\sigma},
> $$
> the same relaxation time that empties the interior of a conductor ([[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]]) — and, like it, independent of the geometry. Driven sinusoidally, the element is resistor-like for $\omega\ll\sigma/\epsilon$ and capacitor-like for $\omega\gg\sigma/\epsilon$: a good insulator is one whose $\sigma/\epsilon$ is far below every frequency you care about.

> [!example] Example — concentric spheres in a lossy medium (HW4 #4)
> Shells of radii $a = 1$ m and $b\to\infty$ in a medium with $\epsilon = 2\epsilon_0$ and $\sigma = 2\times10^{-6}$ S/m. $C = 4\pi\epsilon a = 8\pi\epsilon_0 \approx 222$ pF; $G = (\sigma/\epsilon)C = 8\pi\times10^{-6}\ \text{S} \approx 25\ \mu$S (the shell-by-shell $\int dR$ gives the same); $\tau = \epsilon/\sigma = 8.85\ \mu$s. Given $Q(0) = 1$ C on the inner sphere with nothing connected, $Q(t) = e^{-t/(8.85\,\mu\text{s})}$ C: the charge flows outward through the medium until the sphere is neutral. No return wire is needed — the medium *is* the return path.

## 7. A nonlinear capacitor: the diode junction

The pn junction of [[1-electrostatics/07-poisson-and-laplace#3-poissons-equation-where-there-is-charge|Lecture 7]] stores charge $\pm Q = \pm\rho_2W_2A$ in its two depletion layers, with a potential difference $V = V_{21} = \dfrac{\rho_2W_2(W_1+W_2)}{2\epsilon_0}$ across them. Eliminating the widths with $\rho_1W_1 = \rho_2W_2$ gives $W_1 + W_2 = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\epsilon_0V(\rho_1+\rho_2)}{\rho_1\rho_2}}$ — the depletion region *widens* as the voltage grows — and therefore

$$
Q = A\sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_0\rho_1\rho_2}{\rho_1+\rho_2}}\;\sqrt{V}:
$$

charge grows as $\sqrt V$, not linearly. There is no single $C$; what circuit theory uses is the **small-signal capacitance**, the slope of the $Q$–$V$ curve:

$$
C \equiv \frac{dQ}{dV} = A\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_0\,\rho_1\rho_2}{2V(\rho_1+\rho_2)}} = \frac{\epsilon_0A}{W_1+W_2}\ \propto\ V^{-1/2}.
$$

The last form is the parallel-plate formula with the depletion width as the gap — a nice check, and the reason varactor diodes work as voltage-controlled capacitors. (Using the secant $Q/V$ instead of the slope would give exactly twice this value; for a linear capacitor the two coincide, which is why nobody notices the difference until a nonlinear one shows up. In silicon, replace $\epsilon_0$ by $\epsilon\approx11.7\epsilon_0$.)

## 8. Summary

- $C = Q/V$ with $V$ the drop from $+$ to $-$; computed by a chain, not a formula: $V\to E\to D\to\rho_s\to Q$ or $Q\to D\to E\to V$.
- Plates $\epsilon A/d$; coax $2\pi\epsilon L/\ln(b/a)$, $\mathcal{C} = 2\pi\epsilon/\ln(b/a)$; spheres $4\pi\epsilon ab/(b-a)$.
- Layers crossed in succession add in series ($D$ common); layers side by side add in parallel ($E$ common).
- $W = \tfrac12CV^2$; field energy density $w = \tfrac12\epsilon E^2$ [J/m³].
- Lossy filling: $G = (\sigma/\epsilon)C$, $I = C\,dV/dt + GV$, $\tau = RC = \epsilon/\sigma$.
- Diode: $Q\propto\sqrt V$, $C = dQ/dV = \epsilon_0A/(W_1+W_2)$.
