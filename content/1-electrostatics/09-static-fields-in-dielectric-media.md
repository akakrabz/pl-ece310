*Lecture 9 · course notes §9 · slides "Dielectrics" (examples) · prev: [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]] · next: [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance|Lecture 10]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Lecture 8 produced the machinery; this lecture is about *using* it. Every dielectric problem in the course reduces to a short chain: the **free** charge fixes $\mathbf{D}$ (through Gauss's law and the continuity of normal $\mathbf{D}$), the permittivity of each region converts it to $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon$, a line integral of $\mathbf{E}$ gives the potential, and $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{D}-\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ falls out at the end if anyone asks. The one thing to unlearn is the reflex to reach for Laplace's equation: it is valid only where $\epsilon$ is constant, so layered media are solved layer by layer and stitched with boundary conditions, and continuously varying media must be solved with Gauss's law itself.

## 1. The rules

> [!key] What you may assume in a problem with dielectrics
> 1. $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}$ in each region, with $\epsilon = \epsilon_r\epsilon_0$ given.
> 2. At an interface between two perfect dielectrics there is no free surface charge (unless the problem deliberately puts one there), so **normal $\mathbf{D}$ is continuous**; **tangential $\mathbf{E}$ is continuous** at every interface, always.
> 3. At a conductor–dielectric interface, $\hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_s$ with $\hat{n}$ pointing **from the conductor into the dielectric** and $\mathbf{D}$ evaluated on the dielectric side ($\mathbf{D} = 0$ in the conductor).
> 4. Gauss's law, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$ and $\oint\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{\text{enc}}$, counts **free** charge only — often zero inside a perfect dielectric.
> 5. When $\mathbf{P}$ is wanted: $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{D} - \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} = (\epsilon_r - 1)\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$. Bound surface charge on a dielectric face with outward normal $\hat{n}$: $\rho_{sb} = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{n}$.

> [!recipe] The planar chain (works whenever the geometry has one coordinate)
> $D$ is constant across any charge-free layer ⟶ read its value off a conductor with $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$, or off the enclosed free charge ⟶ $E = D/\epsilon$ in each layer ⟶ $V$ by $-\int E\,dz$, adding up layer by layer ⟶ $P = D - \epsilon_0E$ if required. The same chain runs in cylindrical ($D\propto1/r$) and spherical ($D\propto1/r^2$) symmetry.

> [!example] Example — a slab in a known exterior field
> A dielectric slab, faces perpendicular to $\hat{x}$, sits in free space where $\mathbf{E} = 18\hat{x}$ V/m; inside, the field is measured to be $3\hat{x}$ V/m. Find $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ everywhere, and the slab's $\epsilon_r$ and $\chi_e$.
>
> *Outside:* $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} = 18\epsilon_0\hat{x}$ C/m², $\mathbf{P} = 0$. *Inside:* the field is normal to the faces and the faces carry no free charge, so normal $\mathbf{D}$ is continuous: $\mathbf{D} = 18\epsilon_0\hat{x}$ inside too. Then $\epsilon = D/E = 18\epsilon_0/3 = 6\epsilon_0$: $\epsilon_r = 6$, $\chi_e = \epsilon_r - 1 = 5$. And $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{D} - \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} = 18\epsilon_0\hat{x} - 3\epsilon_0\hat{x} = 15\epsilon_0\hat{x}$ C/m² (cross-check: $\epsilon_0\chi_eE = 5\epsilon_0\cdot3 = 15\epsilon_0$ ✓).
>
> The bound surface charges are $\rho_{sb} = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{n} = +15\epsilon_0$ on the $+x$ face and $-15\epsilon_0$ on the $-x$ face. As a pair of sheets they produce $-15$ V/m between themselves: $18 - 15 = 3$ ✓. Without the number 3 you could not have found $\epsilon_r$ — the measurement is what identifies the material.

> [!key] Field lines bend at a dielectric interface
> $$
> E_{2t} = E_{1t},\qquad \epsilon_2E_{2n} = \epsilon_1E_{1n}\ \Rightarrow\ E_{2n} = \frac{\epsilon_1}{\epsilon_2}E_{1n},
> \qquad\qquad \frac{\tan\theta_1}{\tan\theta_2} = \frac{\epsilon_1}{\epsilon_2}
> $$
> (angles measured from the normal). Crossing into the higher-$\epsilon$ medium the normal component shrinks and the line tilts toward the surface. $\mathbf{D}$, being parallel to $\mathbf{E}$ in each medium, bends by the same angle, but the roles of its components are swapped: its normal part is continuous and its tangential part scales with $\epsilon$. With a free surface charge on the interface, $D_{1n} - D_{2n} = \rho_s$ replaces the second condition ($\hat{n}$ from 2 into 1). The bound surface charge left at the interface is $\rho_{sb} = P_{2n} - P_{1n} = \epsilon_0(E_{1n} - E_{2n})$ (with $\rho_s = 0$) — the jump in $\epsilon_0E_n$ that the free charge does not account for.

This is exactly FA26 Exam 1 problem 3, worked with different numbers in [[problems/fields-across-a-dielectric-interface]]. The full checklist of errors — normal $\mathbf{E}$ made continuous, tangential $\mathbf{D}$ made continuous, ratio inverted, $\epsilon_0$ used for $\epsilon$ — is there.

## 3. Laplace's equation, layer by layer

Poisson's equation was derived by pulling $\epsilon$ out of $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon\nabla V) = -\rho$; that step needs $\epsilon$ independent of position. So:

> [!trap] Do not use Laplace's or Poisson's equation in an inhomogeneous medium
> A medium is **homogeneous** if $\epsilon_r$ is the same everywhere in it. Across an interface between two dielectrics $\epsilon$ jumps, and at the interface $\nabla^2V\ne0$ even with no free charge there — the kink in $V$ carries a *bound* sheet $\rho_{sb}$, and in vacuum language $\nabla^2V = -\rho_{sb}/\epsilon_0$. The fix for **piecewise** homogeneous media is to solve $\nabla^2V=0$ separately in each layer and join the pieces with the boundary conditions: $V$ continuous and $D_n$ continuous. For a **continuously** varying $\epsilon(\mathbf{r})$ there is no fix; go back to Gauss's law.

> [!example] Example — two layers between charged plates
> Conducting plates at $z=0$ (carrying $-2\epsilon_0$ C/m², held at $V=0$) and $z=2$ m (carrying $+2\epsilon_0$ C/m²). The layer $0<z<1$ m has $\epsilon_1 = \epsilon_0$ and $1<z<2$ m has $\epsilon_2 = 2\epsilon_0$. Find $V(2)$.
>
> *Laplace, piecewise.* $V_1 = Az$ in the lower layer (satisfies $V'' = 0$ and $V(0)=0$); $V_2 = A + B(z-1)$ in the upper layer (continuous at $z=1$ by construction). Two unknowns, two conditions:
> - bottom plate, $\hat{n} = +\hat{z}$ from conductor into dielectric: $\rho_s = \hat{z}\cdot\mathbf{D}_1(0) = \epsilon_1\cdot(-A) = -2\epsilon_0$, so $A = 2$;
> - interface, $D_n$ continuous: $\epsilon_1(-A) = \epsilon_2(-B)$, so $B = A\epsilon_1/\epsilon_2 = 1$.
>
> Hence $V = 2z$ on $(0,1)$, $V = 2 + (z-1)$ on $(1,2)$, and $V(2) = 3$ V. The fields are $\mathbf{E} = -2\hat{z}$ and $-\hat{z}$ V/m (pointing from the positive top plate to the negative bottom one, high to low potential), $\mathbf{D} = -2\epsilon_0\hat{z}$ in both layers. The top plate's charge, never used, checks: $-\hat{z}\cdot\mathbf{D}_2(2) = +2\epsilon_0$ ✓.
>
> *$\mathbf{D}$ first (the fast way).* Two sheets $\pm2\epsilon_0$ give $\mathbf{D} = -2\epsilon_0\hat{z}$ between them regardless of what fills the gap. Then $E = D/\epsilon$ in each layer, and $V(2) - V(0) = -\int_0^2E_z\,dz = -[(-2)(1) + (-1)(1)] = 3$ V. Same answer, no unknowns. (This shortcut relies on planar symmetry, where the free sheets alone fix $\mathbf{D}$.)
>
> In general, for a plate at $V_p$ over layers $\epsilon_1$ (thickness $d$) and $\epsilon_2$ (thickness $z_0 - d$), the field in layer 1 is $E_1 = \dfrac{V_p/\epsilon_1}{d/\epsilon_1 + (z_0-d)/\epsilon_2}$: the voltage divides in proportion to $d_i/\epsilon_i$, like two capacitors in series ([[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance#4-layered-dielectrics-series-and-parallel|Lecture 10 §4]]). HW4 #1 and #3 are this problem with the numbers rearranged.

> [!example] Example — a charged sheet between grounded plates
> Both plates ($z=0$ and $z=d$) are grounded; a free sheet $\rho_s$ sits at $z=d_1$ in vacuum, with $d_2 = d - d_1$. Let $V_0 = V(d_1)$. Laplace in each gap gives a "tent": $V = V_0z/d_1$ below the sheet and $V_0(d-z)/d_2$ above, so $\mathbf{E} = -\hat{z}V_0/d_1$ below and $+\hat{z}V_0/d_2$ above. The jump condition at the sheet ($\hat{n} = \hat{z}$, medium 1 above):
> $$
> \epsilon_0\frac{V_0}{d_2} - \Big(-\epsilon_0\frac{V_0}{d_1}\Big) = \rho_s\quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_0 = \frac{\rho_s}{\epsilon_0}\,\frac{d_1d_2}{d}.
> $$
> The plate charges follow from $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$ with $\hat{n}$ into the gap: $\rho_{s}(0) = \hat{z}\cdot\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}(0^+) = -\rho_s\,d_2/d$ and $\rho_s(d) = -\hat{z}\cdot\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}(d^-) = -\rho_s\,d_1/d$. They sum to $-\rho_s$ (the plates together carry the image of the sheet), and in vacuum the *nearer* plate gets the larger share. For $d_1 = d/2$ each plate carries $-\rho_s/2$. With a dielectric $\epsilon_2 = 2\epsilon_0$ above the sheet, $\epsilon_0$ below, plates at $0$ and $5$ m and the sheet $6\epsilon_0$ C/m² at $2$ m (HW4 #2), the same steps give $V_0 = 36/7$ V, $\rho_s(0) = -\tfrac{18}{7}\epsilon_0$ and $\rho_s(5) = -\tfrac{24}{7}\epsilon_0$ C/m². With dielectrics the shares go as $\epsilon_i/d_i$ rather than $1/d_i$, which is why the *farther* plate, under the $2\epsilon_0$ layer, gets more here. (If $\rho_s$ varies slowly in time these are the **quasi-static** fields, valid while $d\ll c/f$.)

## 4. When ε varies continuously: Gauss's law directly

> [!example] Example — a graded dielectric
> Plates at $z=0$ ($V=0$, carrying $\rho_s = 2\epsilon_0$ C/m²) and $z=2$ m bound a dielectric with $\epsilon(z) = \dfrac{4\epsilon_0}{4-z}$ (rising from $\epsilon_0$ at the bottom to $2\epsilon_0$ at the top). Find $V(2)$.
>
> Laplace's equation is off the table. But there is no free charge inside, so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \dfrac{d}{dz}\big(\epsilon E_z\big) = 0$: **$D_z$ is constant**, and the bottom plate fixes it, $D_z = \hat{z}\cdot\mathbf{D}(0) = \rho_s = 2\epsilon_0$. Therefore
> $$
> E_z(z) = \frac{D_z}{\epsilon(z)} = \frac{2\epsilon_0(4-z)}{4\epsilon_0} = 2\Big(1-\frac{z}{4}\Big)\ \text{V/m},
> \qquad
> V(2) = V(0) - \int_0^2 2\Big(1-\frac{z}{4}\Big)dz = -2\Big[z - \frac{z^2}{8}\Big]_0^2 = -3\ \text{V}.
> $$
> $\mathbf{E}$ points $+\hat{z}$ from the positive bottom plate toward the top, so the top plate is at lower potential. The graded medium also carries bound charge: $P_z = D_z - \epsilon_0E_z = \epsilon_0z/2$, so $\rho_b = -dP_z/dz = -\epsilon_0/2$ C/m³ throughout, with $\rho_{sb} = +\epsilon_0$ on the top face and $0$ on the bottom — total bound charge zero, as it must be. In a *uniform* dielectric with no free charge in its bulk, $\rho_b = 0$ (free charge embedded in a uniform dielectric drags along $\rho_b = -(\chi_e/\epsilon_r)\rho$ of the opposite sign); a *graded* $\epsilon$ produces $\rho_b$ even with no free charge at all.
>
> Compare with the two-layer example: both have $\int_0^2dz/\epsilon_r = 3/2$ and both give $\lvert V(2)\rvert = 3$ V. Only the profile of $\epsilon$ along the path matters, because $V = -\int(D/\epsilon)\,dz$ with $D$ constant.

## 5. Spherical symmetry: a charged sphere with a dielectric coat

> [!example] Example — metal sphere, dielectric shell (HW3 #7)
> A metal sphere of radius $a$ carries free charge $Q$; a linear dielectric with permittivity $\epsilon$ (susceptibility $\chi_e$) fills $a<r<b$; free space beyond. Gauss's law for free charge with a spherical surface gives, for every $r>a$,
> $$
> \mathbf{D} = \frac{Q}{4\pi r^2}\,\hat{r},\qquad
> \mathbf{E} = \begin{cases}0, & r<a\\[4pt] \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon r^2}\hat{r}, & a<r<b\\[8pt] \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}\hat{r}, & r>b .\end{cases}
> $$
> The potential at the centre, relative to infinity, is the sum of the drops across the three regions ($\mathbf{E}=0$ in the metal contributes nothing):
> $$
> V(0) = \int_b^\infty\frac{Q\,dr}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2} + \int_a^b\frac{Q\,dr}{4\pi\epsilon r^2} = \frac{Q}{4\pi}\Big(\frac{1}{\epsilon_0b} + \frac{1}{\epsilon a} - \frac{1}{\epsilon b}\Big).
> $$
> In the shell, $\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E} = \Big(1-\dfrac1{\epsilon_r}\Big)\dfrac{Q}{4\pi r^2}\hat{r}$, and $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P} = 0$ because $r^2P_r$ is constant. The bound charge is all on the two surfaces: $\rho_{sb}(a) = \mathbf{P}\cdot(-\hat{r}) = -\Big(1-\dfrac1{\epsilon_r}\Big)\dfrac{Q}{4\pi a^2}$ and $\rho_{sb}(b) = +\Big(1-\dfrac1{\epsilon_r}\Big)\dfrac{Q}{4\pi b^2}$ — totals $\mp(1-1/\epsilon_r)Q$, net zero. The negative layer hugging the metal is what weakens the field in the shell from $Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$ to $Q/(4\pi\epsilon r^2)$: the dielectric is an *imperfect* conductor, cancelling the fraction $1 - 1/\epsilon_r$ of the field where a metal shell would cancel all of it.

> [!example] Example — which conductor is at the higher potential? (concept check)
> A spherical capacitor: PEC for $r<a$, dielectric $3\epsilon_0$ for $a<r<b$, PEC for $r>b$, equal and opposite charges, and $\mathbf{D}$ points radially **inward**. Then the inner conductor is negative, $\mathbf{E}$ points inward too, and $V(b) - V(a) = -\int_a^bE_r\,dr>0$: **$V(b)>V(a)$**. Potential rises when you walk against the field. The value of $\epsilon$ is irrelevant to the sign.

## 6. Summary

- $\mathbf{D}$ from free charge (Gauss, $D_n$ continuous); $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon$ per region; $V = -\int\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ summed over regions; $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{D}-\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ last.
- Interfaces: $E_t$ continuous, $D_n$ continuous (no free charge) ⇒ $\tan\theta_1/\tan\theta_2 = \epsilon_1/\epsilon_2$.
- Laplace/Poisson only inside a homogeneous region; layered ⇒ piecewise plus matching ($V$ and $D_n$); graded ⇒ $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = 0$ directly ($D_z$ constant in planar problems; $rD_r$ or $r^2D_r$ constant in cylindrical or spherical ones).
- Bound charge: $\rho_{sb} = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{n}$ on faces, $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$ wherever $\mathbf{P}$ has divergence (graded $\epsilon$, or free charge inside a dielectric); total zero.
