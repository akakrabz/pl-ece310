*Lecture 4 · course notes §4 · slides "Divergence and curl, Maxwell's equations in differential form" · prev: [[1-electrostatics/03-gauss-law-at-work|Lecture 3]] · next: [[1-electrostatics/05-curl-free-fields-and-the-electrostatic-potential|Lecture 5]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Gauss's law holds for *every* closed surface, including a vanishingly small box around a single point. Divide by the box's volume and you get a local statement: the **divergence** of $\mathbf{D}$ equals the charge density *at that point*, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$. The same move applied to a tiny loop turns a circulation $\oint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ into the **curl**. Divergence measures "flux per unit volume" (sources), curl measures "circulation per unit area" (whirlpools). The divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem let you switch between the integral laws of Lectures 2–3 and these differential laws at will — and they show that static electric fields have zero curl, which is what makes voltage possible (Lecture 5).

## 1. From the fundamental theorem of calculus to Stokes

One idea underlies everything today: *chop a region into tiny pieces, add up a local quantity in each piece, and the interior contributions cancel, leaving only the boundary.*

- **1-D**: $\displaystyle\int_a^b \frac{df}{dx}\,dx = f(b)-f(a)$. Adding up all the little changes $df$ gives the total change.
- **Along a curve** (gradient theorem): $\displaystyle\int_a^b \nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{l} = f(b) - f(a)$, where $\nabla f = \hat{x}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\hat{y}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}+\hat{z}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z}$ is the gradient (direction of steepest increase; $df = \nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{l}$). The answer depends only on the endpoints, so around any *closed* loop $\oint_C\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$: a gradient field is **conservative**, or **curl-free**.
- **Over a surface** (Stokes) and **through a volume** (divergence theorem): the 2-D and 3-D versions, below.

Notation: $\nabla$ ("del") is the vector of partial derivatives, $\nabla = \left(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x},\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y},\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)$. Dotting it into a vector field gives the divergence; crossing it gives the curl. Full formulas on [[0-toolkit/02-vector-calculus-cheatsheet]].

## 2. Divergence: flux per unit volume

Apply Gauss's law, $\oint_S\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_V\rho\,dV$, to a tiny box $\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z$ centred at $(x,y,z)$. The flux out of the pair of faces perpendicular to $\hat{x}$ is

$$
\Big[D_x\big(x+\tfrac{\Delta x}{2}\big) - D_x\big(x-\tfrac{\Delta x}{2}\big)\Big]\Delta y\,\Delta z \approx \frac{\partial D_x}{\partial x}\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z ,
$$

and likewise for the other two pairs. The enclosed charge is $\rho\,\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z$. Divide by the volume and let it shrink:

> [!key] Divergence, and Gauss's law in differential form
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} \equiv \frac{\partial D_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial D_y}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial D_z}{\partial z}
> = \lim_{\Delta V\to 0}\frac{\oint\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S}}{\Delta V},
> \qquad\boxed{\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho\ }
> $$
> The divergence is a **scalar**: the net outward flux per unit volume at a point. Where $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}>0$ the point is a *source* (a faucet; field lines start there); where it is negative, a *sink* (a drain); zero means whatever flows in flows out.

> [!intuition] "Variation along the flow"
> For a field whose arrows all point the same way, $\mathbf{F} = F(x,y,z)\,\hat{u}$ with $\hat{u}$ fixed, divergence is nonzero exactly when $F$ changes *along* $\hat{u}$: arrows pointing $+x$ that grow with $x$ (more leaving the right face than entering the left). Arrows that change only *across* their direction have zero divergence. This is the instructor's rule of thumb — see the figure in §4. When the field lines themselves spread, converge or curve, the geometry contributes too: the Coulomb field $Q\hat{r}/r^2$ weakens along its own direction yet has *zero* divergence off the charge (the spreading exactly compensates). When in doubt, use the formula.

> [!example] Two quick divergences
> 1. $\mathbf{D} = 5x\,\hat{x} + 12\,\hat{y}$ C/m²: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = 5 + 0 + 0 = 5$ C/m³. A uniform positive charge density fills all space — a "faucet of strength 5" everywhere. (The constant $\hat{y}$ part contributes nothing.)
> 2. The pn-junction field of Lecture 3, $E_x = -\rho_1(x+W_1)/\epsilon_0$ on $(-W_1,0)$ and $\rho_2(x-W_2)/\epsilon_0$ on $(0,W_2)$: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \partial E_x/\partial x = -\rho_1/\epsilon_0$ and $+\rho_2/\epsilon_0$ respectively, zero outside. Differentiating the field gives back the charge that produced it — Gauss's law, checked.

The same shrinking argument applied to $\oint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0$ gives the differential form of the second Maxwell equation, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$: the magnetic field has no sources or sinks anywhere.

### The divergence theorem

Tile a volume $V$ with tiny boxes. Flux through a face shared by two boxes is counted once as "out" of one and once as "out" of the other — opposite signs, so it cancels. Only the outer boundary survives:

> [!key] Divergence theorem
> $$
> \int_V \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\,dV = \oint_{S}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\qquad (S = \partial V,\ d\mathbf{S}\ \text{outward})
> $$
> It converts the integral Gauss's law into the differential one and back: $\oint\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}\,dV = \int_V\rho\,dV$ for *every* $V$ forces $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$ pointwise.

## 3. Curl: circulation per unit area

Now the loop version. The **circulation** of a field around a closed curve $C$ is $\oint_C\mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$. Shrink the loop to a point and divide by the enclosed area; the limit depends on the loop's orientation, so it is a **vector**, defined component by component:

> [!key] Curl
> $$
> (\nabla\times\mathbf{v})\cdot\hat{n} = \lim_{\Delta S\to 0}\frac{\oint_C \mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{l}}{\Delta S}
> \qquad(\text{$C$ bounds $\Delta S$, right-hand rule between $d\mathbf{l}$ and $\hat{n}$})
> $$
> In Cartesian components,
> $$
> \nabla\times\mathbf{v} =
> \begin{vmatrix}\hat{x}&\hat{y}&\hat{z}\\[2pt] \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}&\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}&\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\\[6pt] v_x&v_y&v_z\end{vmatrix}
> = \hat{x}\Big(\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial z}\Big)
> + \hat{y}\Big(\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial x}\Big)
> + \hat{z}\Big(\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\Big).
> $$

Read the $\hat{z}$-component off a small rectangle in the $xy$-plane with corners 1–2–3–4: going around, the $v_y$ legs contribute $[v_y(\text{right}) - v_y(\text{left})]\Delta y \approx \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial x}\Delta x\Delta y$ and the $v_x$ legs contribute $-\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\Delta x\Delta y$. Curl at a point means the line integral from 1 to 3 differs along path 1→2→3 and path 1→4→3 — the field is *path-dependent* there.

> [!intuition] "Variation across the flow"
> For a unidirectional field, curl is nonzero exactly when the field changes *across its own direction*: arrows pointing $+y$ that grow with $x$. A paddlewheel dropped in spins because the right side is pushed harder than the left. A uniform stream, however fast, has no curl; a straight shear flow does, even though nothing goes "around". Curved flow adds a geometric part: rigid rotation ($\mathbf{v}\propto r\hat{\phi}$) has curl even though the speed is the same on both sides of a small wheel, while the free vortex $\mathbf{v}\propto\hat{\phi}/r$ — the shape of the magnetic field around a wire — has *zero* curl everywhere except on its axis. The spin axis, by the right-hand rule, is the direction of $\nabla\times\mathbf{v}$.

> [!example] A whirlpool of strength 5
> $\mathbf{v} = -3y\,\hat{x} + 2x\,\hat{y}$. Only the $\hat{z}$-component can be nonzero:
> $\nabla\times\mathbf{v} = \hat{z}\left(\dfrac{\partial(2x)}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial(-3y)}{\partial y}\right) = \hat{z}\,(2+3) = 5\hat{z}$.
> Uniform: the same circulation per unit area everywhere. (Compare $\mathbf{v} = 3x\hat{x}+2y\hat{y}$, a "faucet of strength 5" with zero curl.)

> [!trap] Determinant sign errors — even solution keys make them
> The middle term carries a **minus** when you expand along the top row, and the $\hat{z}$ term is $\partial_x v_y - \partial_y v_x$ (in that order). Writing that bracket backwards is a slip that turns up even in official solution keys; it goes unpunished only when the two partials happen to be equal. Write the determinant, expand it mechanically, and then *check one component with the "across the flow" picture*.

> [!key] Stokes' theorem
> $$
> \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot d\mathbf{S}\qquad (C = \partial S,\ \text{right-hand rule})
> $$
> Circulation around the boundary = total curl through the surface. Any surface bounded by $C$ gives the same answer.

## 5. Conservative fields

Four statements about a vector field are equivalent (each implies the others, on all of space or on any region without holes):

1. $\nabla\times\mathbf{v} = 0$ everywhere — **curl-free**;
2. $\oint_C\mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ around every closed loop (by Stokes);
3. $\int_a^b\mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is **path-independent**;
4. $\mathbf{v} = -\nabla f$ for some scalar function $f$ — the field is a gradient (a **potential** exists).

The minus sign in (4) is a convention chosen so that, for $\mathbf{E}$, $f$ is the familiar voltage. Lecture 5 is entirely about exploiting (4). Concept page: [[concepts/conservative-field]].

## 6. Static electric fields are curl-free

Take the curl of the Coulomb field of a point charge, $\mathbf{E} = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{\mathbf{r}}{r^3}$. Each component is of the form $x\,g(r)$ with $g = (4\pi\epsilon_0)^{-1}Q\,r^{-3}$, and

$$
(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})_z = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(y\,g(r)\big) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\big(x\,g(r)\big)
= y\,g'(r)\frac{x}{r} - x\,g'(r)\frac{y}{r} = 0 ,
$$

and the same for the other components: **every radial field $g(r)\,\mathbf{r}$ is curl-free.** Because curl is a linear operation and every static field is a superposition of Coulomb fields, **all electrostatic fields are curl-free, without exception**. (The notes check the infinite-line field $\dfrac{\rho_l}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{x\hat{x}+y\hat{y}}{x^2+y^2}$ the same way: $\partial_x\!\left(\dfrac{y}{x^2+y^2}\right) - \partial_y\!\left(\dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right) = \dfrac{-2xy+2xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = 0$.)

> [!key] The laws of electrostatics
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho,\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0,\qquad \mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}\ (\text{vacuum}).
> $$
> By the **Helmholtz theorem**, a vector field that vanishes at infinity is uniquely determined by its divergence and its curl. So these two equations *are* electrostatics: given $\rho$, the field is fixed. Lecture 5 uses $\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=0$ to write $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$; Lecture 7 combines both into Poisson's equation $\nabla^2 V = -\rho/\epsilon_0$.

## 7. The differential form of Maxwell's equations

Each integral law becomes a local one by the theorem that matches its geometry — Stokes for the curl equations, the divergence theorem for the divergence equations — plus the argument "true for every surface/volume ⇒ true pointwise":

| integral form | theorem | differential form |
|---|---|---|
| $\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\displaystyle\int_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ (Faraday) | Stokes | $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$ |
| $\oint_C\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \displaystyle\int_S\Big(\mathbf{J}+\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\Big)\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ (Ampère–Maxwell) | Stokes | $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}+\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}$ |
| $\oint_S\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \displaystyle\int_V\rho\,dV$ (Gauss) | divergence | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$ |
| $\oint_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0$ | divergence | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ |

Physical names: $\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is an **EMF** (volts) and $\oint_C\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ an **MMF** (amperes). Ampère's right-hand side has a "static" part, the **conduction current** $\mathbf{J}$, and a "dynamic" part, the **displacement current** $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ — Maxwell's addition, and the reason light exists (Lecture 16 onward).

> [!trap] Partial, not total, time derivatives
> Fields depend on $(x,y,z,t)$; the differential equations use $\partial/\partial t$. Some slides write $d/dt$ — read it as $\partial/\partial t$.

### A free consequence: charge is conserved

Take the divergence of the Ampère–Maxwell equation. Since $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{H}) = 0$ identically,

$$
0 = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = -\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\ }
$$

the **continuity equation**: current flowing out of a point drains the charge there. Charge conservation is built into Maxwell's equations. Three identities used here and often, valid for any smooth fields:

$$
\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{A}) = 0,\qquad
\nabla\times(\nabla f) = 0,\qquad
\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{A} = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}) - \nabla^2\mathbf{A},\qquad \nabla^2\mathbf{A}\equiv\hat{x}\nabla^2A_x+\hat{y}\nabla^2A_y+\hat{z}\nabla^2A_z .
$$

(The identity holds in any coordinates; the component-wise Laplacian on the right is valid for *Cartesian* components only.)

Concept page: [[concepts/maxwells-equations]].

## 8. Summary

- Divergence = flux per unit volume (scalar, "along the flow"); $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$.
- Curl = circulation per unit area (vector, "across the flow"); Faraday and Ampère are curl equations.
- Divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem: local ↔ integral, both proved by "interior contributions cancel".
- Static $\mathbf{E}$ is curl-free ⇒ conservative ⇒ a potential exists (Lecture 5).
- Continuity, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = -\partial\rho/\partial t$, is contained in Maxwell's equations.
