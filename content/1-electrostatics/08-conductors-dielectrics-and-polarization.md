*Lecture 8 · course notes §8 · slides "Conductors" and "Dielectrics" · prev: [[1-electrostatics/07-poisson-and-laplace|Lecture 7]] · next: [[1-electrostatics/09-static-fields-in-dielectric-media|Lecture 9]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> So far every charge sat in empty space. Real problems have *stuff* in them, and stuff is made of charges that respond to the field. If the charges are **free** to move (a conductor), they move until there is nothing left to push them: the interior field is zero, the body is an equipotential, and all the charge ends up on the surface. If the charges are **bound** (a dielectric), they can only shift a little, turning each atom into a tiny dipole; the sum of those dipoles is the **polarization** $\mathbf{P}$, whose own field opposes the applied one and *weakens* the field inside without cancelling it. The bookkeeping trick $\mathbf{D} \equiv \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}$ hides the bound charge inside $\mathbf{D}$, so that Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$ needs only the charge you put there yourself. For ordinary materials $\mathbf{P}\propto\mathbf{E}$ and the whole story collapses into one number: $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$.

## 1. Two ideal materials

A material's electrical character is summarized by its **conductivity** $\sigma$ [S/m], the constant in the point form of Ohm's law,

> [!key] Ohm's law (the only Ohm's law this course uses)
> $$
> \mathbf{J} = \sigma\,\mathbf{E}\qquad[\text{A/m}^2 = (\text{S/m})(\text{V/m})].
> $$
> Current density is proportional to the field, with the material property $\sigma$ as the constant. The circuit version follows for a block of length $l$ and cross-section $A$: $V = El$, $I = JA$, so $R = V/I = l/(\sigma A)$. Resistivity is $1/\sigma$ [Ω·m]. Real conductors obey this because their carriers keep colliding with the lattice — the drift velocity, and hence $\mathbf{J}$, settles at a value proportional to $\mathbf{E}$ (Lecture 11 builds the model).

Conductivities span more than twenty orders of magnitude at room temperature: copper $\approx 6\times10^{7}$ S/m, sea water $\approx 4$, germanium $\approx 2$, distilled water $\sim10^{-4}$, glass $\sim10^{-12}$, fused quartz $\sim10^{-17}$; superconductors are effectively $\infty$. The two ends of the scale are idealized:

| | limit | consequence | circuit analogy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **perfect electric conductor (PEC)** | $\sigma\to\infty$ | $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside, whatever $\mathbf{J}$ flows | short circuit: $V = 0$ for any $I$ |
| **perfect dielectric (insulator)** | $\sigma\to0$ | $\mathbf{J} = 0$ inside, whatever $\mathbf{E}$ is applied | open circuit: $I = 0$ for any $V$ |

## 2. Conductors in electrostatics

A PEC has $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside by definition. The striking fact is that a *real* conductor reaches the same state in equilibrium, and quickly.

Insert a conducting slab into a uniform field $\mathbf{E}_0$. Free electrons feel $q\mathbf{E}_0$ with $q<0$ and drift *against* the field, piling up on one face and leaving the opposite face positively charged. The slab stays neutral, but its charge is now separated, and the induced pair $\pm\rho_s$ produces a **secondary field** $-\mathbf{E}_0$ inside. Charge keeps moving as long as any interior field remains, so the motion stops exactly when the total interior field is zero. Everything about conductors in statics follows:

> [!key] A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium
> 1. $\mathbf{E} = 0$ everywhere inside.
> 2. $V$ is constant throughout the conductor: the whole body, surface included, is one **equipotential** ($V(b) - V(a) = -\int_a^b\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ for any two of its points).
> 3. $\rho = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = 0$ inside: **all net charge lives on the surface**, as a surface charge density $\rho_s$.
> 4. At the surface, the boundary conditions with zero field inside give $\hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_s$ and $\hat{n}\times\mathbf{E} = 0$, with $\hat{n}$ pointing **out of the conductor**: the field just outside is **perpendicular** to the surface and its normal $\mathbf{D}$ component *is* the local surface charge.
>
> Inside a PEC the course also takes $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B} = 0$ (a static $\mathbf{H}$ *could* exist there, but there is no way to set one up).

> [!derivation]- How fast does a conductor reach equilibrium?
> Combine charge conservation $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$ (Lecture 4), Ohm's law $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$, and Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \rho/\epsilon$ in a homogeneous conductor:
> $$
> \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \sigma\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon}\rho = 0
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> \rho(t) = \rho(0)\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau = \frac{\epsilon}{\sigma} .
> $$
> Any interior charge density decays exponentially with the **relaxation time** $\tau = \epsilon/\sigma$; the charge does not vanish but migrates to the surface. Once the interior is charge-free and no current is being driven through the conductor by an external circuit, $\mathbf{J} = 0$ and hence $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside. (A wire carrying a steady current is the other case: $\rho = 0$ inside but $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{J}/\sigma\neq0$.) For copper with $\epsilon\approx\epsilon_0$, $\tau\sim10^{-19}$ s — so short that the simple model is not even valid on that scale, but the conclusion stands: an isolated metal is in electrostatic equilibrium for any field that varies more slowly than this, which is every field in this course. For sea water ($\epsilon\approx81\epsilon_0$, $\sigma\approx4$ S/m), $\tau\approx0.2$ ns. The same $\tau$ reappears in [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance|Lecture 10]] as $RC = \epsilon/\sigma$ of a leaky capacitor.

> [!example] Example — a neutral conducting slab between two charged sheets
> Sheets $\rho_A>0$ (above) and $\rho_B>0$ (below) sandwich an uncharged conducting slab. Find the induced charges $\rho_1$ (top face) and $\rho_2$ (bottom face).
>
> The slab's induced charges are equal and opposite (it is neutral), so *outside the slab* their fields cancel and the gap fields are those of the two sheets alone: each sheet gives $\rho_s/(2\epsilon_0)$ pointing away from itself, so between the sheets $\mathbf{D} = \tfrac12(\rho_B - \rho_A)\,\hat{z}$ ($\hat{z}$ up). Inside the slab $\mathbf{D} = 0$. Now read the faces with $\hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_s$, $\hat{n}$ pointing out of the metal:
> - top face, $\hat{n} = +\hat{z}$: $\rho_1 = \tfrac12(\rho_B - \rho_A)$;
> - bottom face, $\hat{n} = -\hat{z}$: $\rho_2 = \tfrac12(\rho_A - \rho_B) = -\rho_1$. ✓
>
> Check: the field inside the slab from all four sheets is $\tfrac{1}{2\epsilon_0}(-\rho_A - \rho_1 + \rho_2 + \rho_B) = 0$ ✓, and if $\rho_A = \rho_B$ nothing is induced, as symmetry demands.

> [!example] Example — a point charge above a grounded conducting plane (concept check)
> A positive point charge $Q$ sits above an infinite grounded conducting plane. Which is true? *E is zero above the plane* — no. *E is zero below the plane* — **yes**: the plane shields the other side completely. *The induced charge is uniform* — no: $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$ follows the field, which is strongest directly under $Q$. *The total induced charge is $+Q$* — no: field lines from $Q$ end on negative charge, and all of them end on the plane (none can pass through), so the induced total is $-Q$. *The field has a tangential component at the plane* — no: $\hat{n}\times\mathbf{E} = 0$ at a conductor, so every field line meets the plane at a right angle.

## 3. Dielectrics: bound charge and polarization

In an insulator the electrons are bound to their atoms. An applied field cannot free them, but it can *displace* them slightly relative to the nuclei: each neutral atom becomes a small **dipole** with moment $\mathbf{p} = q\mathbf{d}$ (charge times the displacement from $-$ to $+$), aligned with $\mathbf{E}$. The material is **polarized**.

Inside the slab, the $+$ end of one dipole sits next to the $-$ end of its neighbour, and the charges cancel on average. Only at the two faces is the cancellation incomplete: a layer of uncompensated $+$ charge on the face the dipoles point toward, $-$ on the other. Those **bound surface charges** $\pm\rho_{sb}$ produce a field opposing the applied one. Unlike a conductor's free charge, the bound charge cannot keep moving until the field is zero — each electron is tethered — so the interior field is **reduced, not eliminated**.

> [!key] Polarization
> $$
> \mathbf{P} \equiv N\,\mathbf{p} = \text{dipole moment per unit volume}\qquad[\text{C·m/m}^3 = \text{C/m}^2],
> $$
> where $N$ is the number of dipoles per unit volume. $\mathbf{P}$ has the units of a surface charge density, and that is no accident: on any surface of a polarized body, with outward normal $\hat{n}$, the bound surface charge is
> $$
> \rho_{sb} = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{n},
> $$
> and in general a non-uniform $\mathbf{P}$ leaves behind a bound **volume** charge $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$ (where more dipole "heads" arrive than "tails" leave). Bound charge is real charge and makes real fields; it is just charge you did not place and cannot move.

> [!derivation]- The averaged field of a lattice of dipoles
> Model the dielectric as a lattice with spacings $\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z$, each cell holding one proton–electron pair displaced by $d$ along $\hat{z}$ (so $\mathbf{p} = ed\,\hat{z}$ and $N = 1/(\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z)$). Viewed in $z$, the protons form sheets of density $+e/(\Delta x\Delta y)$ and the electrons sheets of $-e/(\Delta x\Delta y)$, each $+$ sheet a distance $d$ above its $-$ partner. Between the members of a pair the two sheets produce $\mathbf{E}_1 = -\hat{z}\,\frac{e}{\epsilon_0\Delta x\Delta y}$ (opposite to $\mathbf{p}$); between pairs the fields cancel, $\mathbf{E}_2 = 0$. The pairs occupy a fraction $d/\Delta z$ of the volume, so the space-averaged field of the bound charges is
> $$
> \mathbf{E}_p = \mathbf{E}_1\frac{d}{\Delta z} + \mathbf{E}_2\frac{\Delta z - d}{\Delta z} = -\hat{z}\frac{e\,d}{\epsilon_0\,\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z} = -\frac{N\mathbf{p}}{\epsilon_0} = -\frac{\mathbf{P}}{\epsilon_0}.
> $$
> So inside an infinite slab polarized *normal to its faces*, the total field is $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{E}_0 + \mathbf{E}_p = \mathbf{E}_0 - \mathbf{P}/\epsilon_0$, where $\mathbf{E}_0$ is the field the free charges alone would make. Weaker than $\mathbf{E}_0$, since $\mathbf{P}\parallel\mathbf{E}_0$; not zero. (Polarized *along* its faces, a slab has no bound surface charge at all, $\mathbf{E}_p = 0$, and $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{E}_0$ inside.)

## 4. The displacement field D, and Gauss's law in matter

Rearrange the slab result: $\epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}_0$. The right side is what $\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ was *before* the dielectric was inserted — the field of the free charges alone. That suggests a definition:

> [!key] The displacement field and Gauss's law for free charge
> $$
> \mathbf{D} \equiv \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}\qquad[\text{C/m}^2],\qquad\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_f,\qquad \oint_S\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{f,\text{enc}} .
> $$
> Gauss's law in its original form counts *all* charge: $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}) = \rho_f + \rho_b$. Substituting $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$ and moving it to the left gives $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}) = \rho_f$: the bound charge has been absorbed into $\mathbf{D}$. From here on $\rho$ without a subscript means **free** charge, and $\mathbf{D}$ is the field whose sources are the charges you control. In vacuum $\mathbf{P} = 0$ and $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$, as before.

The boundary conditions of Lecture 6 survive unchanged, because they came from the integral laws: $\hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{D}_1-\mathbf{D}_2) = \rho_s$ with $\rho_s$ the **free** surface charge, and $\hat{n}\times(\mathbf{E}_1-\mathbf{E}_2) = 0$ always. Applied to $\mathbf{P}$ the same pillbox gives $\hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{P}_1-\mathbf{P}_2) = -\rho_{sb}$ — the bound surface charge at an interface is the amount by which normal $\mathbf{P}$ *decreases* going from medium 2 into medium 1 (along $\hat{n}$).

> [!trap] "D is the same inside and outside" is a slab result, not a law
> For an infinite slab with the field *normal* to its faces, $\mathbf{D}$ inside equals $\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}_0$ outside because the bound layers on the faces are infinite sheets whose fields cancel outside. With the field *along* the faces, $\mathbf{E}$ is unchanged inside and it is $\mathbf{D}$ that jumps, by $\mathbf{P}$ — the tangential-$\mathbf{D}$ jump of Lecture 6. For a dielectric *sphere* in a uniform field, the bound charge makes a dipole-like fringe field outside and inside gives $\mathbf{E}_p = -\mathbf{P}/(3\epsilon_0)$, not $-\mathbf{P}/\epsilon_0$. What *is* general: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_f$, and the continuity of normal $\mathbf{D}$ across an interface carrying no free charge. In planar and cylindrically or spherically symmetric problems that is enough to get $\mathbf{D}$ from the free charge alone — which is why those are the problems you get.

## 5. Linear dielectrics: χe, εr, and ε

For most materials at ordinary field strengths the polarization is proportional to the total field:

> [!key] Constitutive relation of a linear, isotropic dielectric
> $$
> \mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\,\mathbf{E}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> \mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E} = \epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)\,\mathbf{E} = \epsilon\,\mathbf{E},
> \qquad \epsilon = \epsilon_r\epsilon_0,\quad \epsilon_r = 1+\chi_e .
> $$
> $\chi_e\ge0$ is the dimensionless **electric susceptibility**, $\epsilon_r$ the dimensionless **relative permittivity** (or "dielectric constant"), and $\epsilon$ the **permittivity** [F/m]. The $\mathbf{E}$ in $\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E}$ is the **total** field in the material — applied plus the polarization's own — not the applied field alone. Typical values of $\epsilon_r$: vacuum 1, air 1.0006, glass 4–10, dry-to-wet soil 5–10, silicon 11–12, distilled water 81.

Once $\epsilon$ is known you rarely need $\mathbf{P}$ at all: use $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ and the free charges, and recover $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{D} - \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ afterwards if asked. (Anisotropic crystals, where $\epsilon$ is a tensor and $\mathbf{P}$ need not be parallel to $\mathbf{E}$, are not treated in this course; plasmas, where $\chi_e<0$, are deferred to ECE 350.)

> [!example] Example — the slab between charged plates, three ways
> Sheets $+\rho_{s0}$ (below) and $-\rho_{s0}$ (above) produce an applied field $\mathbf{E}_a = \hat{z}\,\rho_{s0}/\epsilon_0$ in the gap. Fill the gap with a dielectric of susceptibility $\chi_e$.
> - *Self-consistently:* the polarization $P = \epsilon_0\chi_eE$ puts bound charge $\pm\rho_{sb} = \pm P$ on the faces, whose field $E_p = -P/\epsilon_0 = -\chi_eE$ opposes $\mathbf{E}_a$. The total field satisfies $E = E_a - \chi_eE$, so
> $$
> E = \frac{E_a}{1+\chi_e} = \frac{\rho_{s0}}{\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)} = \frac{\rho_{s0}}{\epsilon},\qquad \rho_{sb} = \frac{\chi_e}{1+\chi_e}\,\rho_{s0}.
> $$
> - *Via $\mathbf{D}$:* the free charge has not changed, so $D = \rho_{s0}$ exactly as without the dielectric (Gauss for free charge, or $D_n$ continuous at the faces since they carry no *free* charge), and $E = D/\epsilon$. One line.
> - *Limits:* $\chi_e = 0$ is vacuum ($\mathbf{P}=0$, no bound charge); $\chi_e\to\infty$ drives $E\to0$ — the dielectric behaves more and more like a conductor.
>
> The moral, and the habit for the rest of the unit: **$\mathbf{D}$ is fixed by the free charge; $\mathbf{E}$ is reduced by $\epsilon_r$; $\mathbf{P}$ makes up the difference.**

> [!example] Example — copper plates: vacuum, then a fluid, then salt water (HW4 #5)
> Charged copper plates produce $\mathbf{E} = 3\hat{z}$ V/m in the vacuum gap between them and zero elsewhere. (a) $\mathbf{D} = 3\epsilon_0\hat{z}$ C/m², $\mathbf{P} = 0$. (b) The bottom plate carries $\rho_s = \hat{z}\cdot\mathbf{D} = 3\epsilon_0$ C/m². (c) Fill the gap with an insulating fluid of $\epsilon = 81\epsilon_0$, plate charges unchanged: $\mathbf{D}$ is unchanged, $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon = \tfrac{1}{27}\hat{z}$ V/m, $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{D} - \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} = \tfrac{80}{27}\epsilon_0\hat{z}$ C/m² — the bound charge on the fluid's faces cancels 80/81 of the plates' field. (d) Dissolve salt so the fluid conducts ($\sigma\approx4$ S/m): ions now drift to the plates and keep drifting until the interior field is *zero*. In the steady state $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D} = \mathbf{P} = 0$ in the fluid; the salt water has shorted the capacitor. Conductor versus dielectric, in one experiment.

## 6. Summary

- Conductors: $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$; in equilibrium $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside, $V$ constant, $\rho = 0$ inside, $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{E}\perp$ surface outside. Equilibrium is reached in $\tau = \epsilon/\sigma$.
- Dielectrics: bound charges shift, $\mathbf{P} = N\mathbf{p}$ [C/m²]; bound charges $\rho_{sb} = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{n}$, $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$; field reduced, not zero.
- $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}$ turns Gauss's law into a statement about free charge only; boundary conditions unchanged.
- Linear media: $\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E}$ (total field), $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$, $\epsilon = \epsilon_0(1+\chi_e) = \epsilon_r\epsilon_0$.
