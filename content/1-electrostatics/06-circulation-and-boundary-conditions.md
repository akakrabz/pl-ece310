*Lecture 6 · course notes §6 · slides "Boundary Conditions; Poisson's and Laplace's Equation" (first half) · prev: [[1-electrostatics/05-curl-free-fields-and-the-electrostatic-potential|Lecture 5]] · next: [[1-electrostatics/07-poisson-and-laplace|Lecture 7]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Close the path of Lecture 5 on itself and the line integral of a static field is zero: the **circulation** vanishes, which is Kirchhoff's voltage law and, through Stokes' theorem, the same statement as $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$. The differential laws, though, assume the fields are differentiable — and at the surface of a conductor or a dielectric they are not. Fields *jump* there. The integral laws still hold for a loop or a pillbox that straddles the surface, and letting the loop or pillbox shrink onto the surface turns each Maxwell equation into a **boundary condition**: a rule for how much each field component may jump. Four rules, one drawing, and a normal vector whose direction you must never lose track of.

## 1. Circulation: the loop version of path independence

If the two endpoints of a path coincide, path independence says the integral is zero for *every* closed loop $C$:

> [!key] Circulation of a static field
> $$
> \oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0 .
> $$
> $\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is the **circulation** of $\mathbf{E}$ around $C$. Fields with zero circulation around every loop are **conservative**, and by Stokes' theorem, $\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ for any surface $S$ spanning $C$, "zero circulation everywhere" and "zero curl everywhere" are the same thing. In circuit language this is KVL: the potential drops around a closed loop sum to zero.

> [!example] Example — a rectangle in a slab field
> Inside a uniformly charged slab centred on $x=0$ the field is $\mathbf{E} = \hat{x}\,\rho x/\epsilon_0$ ([[1-electrostatics/03-gauss-law-at-work#3c-uniform-slab-planar-with-interior|Lecture 3]]); let the slab be wide enough ($\lvert x\rvert<W/2$ with $W/2\ge3$ m) to contain the loop. Take the rectangle with corners $(1,0)$, $(3,0)$, $(3,4)$, $(1,4)$ in the $xy$-plane, traversed in that order (counter-clockwise seen from $+z$):
> $$
> \oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \underbrace{\int_{x=1}^{3}\frac{\rho x}{\epsilon_0}\,dx}_{4\rho/\epsilon_0} + \underbrace{\int_{y=0}^{4}\hat{x}\frac{3\rho}{\epsilon_0}\cdot\hat{y}\,dy}_{0} + \underbrace{\int_{x=3}^{1}\frac{\rho x}{\epsilon_0}\,dx}_{-4\rho/\epsilon_0} + \underbrace{\int_{y=4}^{0}\hat{x}\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}\cdot\hat{y}\,dy}_{0} = 0 .
> $$
> The vertical legs contribute nothing because $\mathbf{E}\perp d\mathbf{l}$; the two horizontal legs are equal and opposite. (The notes use the rectangle $-3\le x\le3$, where each horizontal leg is zero on its own; the off-centre version shows the cancellation doing real work.) Bookkeeping to copy: $d\mathbf{l}$ is $\hat{x}\,dx$ or $\hat{y}\,dy$ on each leg, always with the plus sign, and the *limits* are ordered so as to walk around $C$ in the chosen direction.

Curl is the local version of circulation: the $\hat{x}$-component of $\nabla\times\mathbf{E}$ is the circulation per unit area around a tiny loop in a constant-$x$ plane, $\lim\frac{1}{\Delta y\Delta z}\oint_{C_x}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial z}$, and similarly for the other two components ([[1-electrostatics/04-divergence-and-curl#3-curl-circulation-per-unit-area|Lecture 4 §3]]). The right-hand rule fixes the sign: thumb along the direction of travel, fingers through the surface along $d\mathbf{S}$.

> [!key] The laws of electrostatics, and when they apply
> $$
> \nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho,\qquad \mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}\ (\text{vacuum}).
> $$
> They also hold **quasi-statically**: if the sources vary on a time scale $\tau$ much longer than the light-travel time $L/c$ across the region of interest, the fields at each instant are the static fields of the instantaneous sources (with a small, slowly varying $\mathbf{B}$ riding along). For anything in a laboratory driven below a few MHz this is an excellent approximation; the transmission-line unit is about what happens when it fails.

## 2. Where fields jump

The differential laws require the fields to be differentiable. At a **surface charge** they are not: recall the infinite sheet of [[1-electrostatics/03-gauss-law-at-work#3b-infinite-sheet-planar|Lecture 3]], $\mathbf{E} = \hat{z}\dfrac{\rho_s}{2\epsilon_0}\text{sgn}(z)$, whose $z$-component flips sign across the sheet. Now superpose on it whatever smooth field $\mathbf{E}_0$ the *other* sources produce:

$$
\mathbf{E} = \hat{z}\frac{\rho_s}{2\epsilon_0}\text{sgn}(z) + \mathbf{E}_0 .
$$

Just above and just below the sheet ($z=0^\pm$) the smooth part is the same, so the **tangential components** $E_x, E_y$ are continuous, while the **normal component** jumps: $E_z(0^+) - E_z(0^-) = \rho_s/\epsilon_0$, i.e. $D_z(0^+) - D_z(0^-) = \rho_s$. Nothing about this argument used the orientation of the sheet or its flatness (a curved surface is flat on a small enough scale), so the same holds at *any* charged surface. That is the content of the boundary conditions; the next section derives them properly, directly from the integral laws, so that the magnetic ones come for free.

## 3. The boundary conditions, from the integral laws

The integral forms of Maxwell's equations hold for **every** closed surface and closed path — including ones that straddle the boundary between two materials, where the differential forms are meaningless. So apply them to a shape that straddles the interface and then squash it flat.

> [!derivation]- Pillbox and loop, step by step
> **Conventions.** Medium 1 on one side, medium 2 on the other; $\hat{n}$ is the unit normal to the interface **pointing from medium 2 into medium 1**. Subscripts $n$ and $t$ denote normal and tangential components.
>
> **Gauss's law on a pillbox.** Caps of area $A$ parallel to the surface, one in each medium, joined by a side wall of height $h$. As $h\to0$ the side wall's flux vanishes (finite field, vanishing area), the enclosed *volume* charge vanishes, but the *surface* charge $\rho_sA$ inside stays. Outward normals are $+\hat{n}$ on the medium-1 cap and $-\hat{n}$ on the medium-2 cap:
> $$
> \oint\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{D}_1\cdot\hat{n}A - \mathbf{D}_2\cdot\hat{n}A = \rho_sA \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{D}_1-\mathbf{D}_2) = \rho_s .
> $$
> The same pillbox with $\oint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0$ gives $\hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{B}_1-\mathbf{B}_2) = 0$.
>
> **Faraday's law on a loop.** A rectangle of length $L$ along the surface and height $h$ across it, one long edge in each medium. As $h\to0$ the short edges contribute nothing, and the magnetic flux through the loop ($\propto Lh$) vanishes even if $\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t\neq0$. The long edges are traversed in opposite directions:
> $$
> \oint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = E_{1t}L - E_{2t}L = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_{1t} = E_{2t},\ \text{i.e.}\ \hat{n}\times(\mathbf{E}_1-\mathbf{E}_2) = 0 .
> $$
> (The cross product with $\hat{n}$ picks out the tangential part of a vector — rotated by 90°, but zero is zero.)
>
> **Ampère's law on the same loop.** $\oint\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_{\text{enc}} + \frac{d}{dt}\int\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$. The displacement term dies with the area, but a **surface current** $\mathbf{J}_s$ [A/m] flowing *in* the interface threads the loop with $I_{\text{enc}} = J_sL$ (the component of $\mathbf{J}_s$ perpendicular to the loop). Hence $H_{1t} - H_{2t} = J_s$, or in vector form $\hat{n}\times(\mathbf{H}_1-\mathbf{H}_2) = \mathbf{J}_s$.
>
> Because the time-derivative terms vanish with the *area*, not because the fields are static, these four conditions hold for time-varying fields as well.

> [!key] The four boundary conditions ($\hat{n}$ from medium 2 into medium 1)
> $$
> \hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{D}_1-\mathbf{D}_2) = \rho_s,\qquad
> \hat{n}\times(\mathbf{E}_1-\mathbf{E}_2) = 0,\qquad
> \hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{B}_1-\mathbf{B}_2) = 0,\qquad
> \hat{n}\times(\mathbf{H}_1-\mathbf{H}_2) = \mathbf{J}_s .
> $$
> In words: **normal $\mathbf{D}$ jumps by the free surface charge; tangential $\mathbf{E}$ is continuous; normal $\mathbf{B}$ is continuous; tangential $\mathbf{H}$ jumps by the surface current.** The conditions say *nothing* about tangential $\mathbf{D}$ or normal $\mathbf{E}$ (they jump at a dielectric interface even with $\rho_s=0$), nor about normal $\mathbf{H}$ or tangential $\mathbf{B}$. The course notes write the same equations with $\pm$ superscripts, $\hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{D}^+-\mathbf{D}^-) = \rho_s$, where "$+$" is the side $\hat{n}$ points into — identical content.

> [!recipe] How to remember them
> Take each Maxwell equation in differential form and replace $\nabla\to\hat{n}$, each field by (field in 1) − (field in 2), $\rho\to\rho_s$, $\mathbf{J}\to\mathbf{J}_s$, and $\partial/\partial t\to0$. Then $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}=\rho$ becomes $\hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{D}_1-\mathbf{D}_2)=\rho_s$, $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t$ becomes $\hat{n}\times(\mathbf{E}_1-\mathbf{E}_2)=0$, and so on. Divergence equations constrain normal components; curl equations constrain tangential ones.

## 4. The normal vector is half the problem

Every sign error in this topic is an $\hat{n}$ error. The rule "$\hat{n}$ points from 2 into 1" means that *you* choose which side is medium 1 — and then $\hat{n}$ must point into it. At a second interface of the same problem you will usually have to re-choose, and $\hat{n}$ flips. Write $\hat{n}$ on the drawing at every interface before you write an equation.

> [!example] Example — a vacuum gap bounded by two charged planes
> Everything is in vacuum. Measurements give $\mathbf{D} = 0$ for $x<0$. The planes $x=0$ and $x=5$ m carry $\rho_s = 2$ C/m² and an unknown $\rho_{s0}$, and there are no other charges. Find $\mathbf{D}$ everywhere and $\rho_{s0}$.
>
> *At $x=0$:* call the gap $0<x<5$ medium 1 and $x<0$ medium 2, so $\hat{n} = +\hat{x}$. Then $\hat{x}\cdot(\mathbf{D}_1 - 0) = 2$, so $D_{1x} = 2$; there is no tangential field on the left, $E_t$ is continuous, and $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ on both sides, so $\mathbf{D}_1 = 2\hat{x}$ C/m² in the gap.
>
> *At $x=5$:* now the gap is the "2" side and $x>5$ is medium 1 (so $\hat{n} = +\hat{x}$ again pointing into $x>5$) — or, equivalently, keep the gap as medium 1 and take $\hat{n} = -\hat{x}$ pointing into it from the right. Either way: $D_x(x>5) - 2 = \rho_{s0}$. The jump condition alone cannot fix $\rho_{s0}$; superposition does. Two sheets are the only sources, and their fields must cancel for $x<0$: sheet 1 contributes $-\tfrac{2}{2}\hat{x}$ there and sheet 2 contributes $-\tfrac{\rho_{s0}}{2}\hat{x}$, so $\rho_{s0} = -2$ C/m², and then $\mathbf{D} = 0$ for $x>5$ as well.
>
> Result: $\mathbf{D} = 0,\ 2\hat{x},\ 0$ C/m² in the three regions — a parallel-plate capacitor's field.

> [!example] Example — tangential and normal bookkeeping together
> Now $\mathbf{D} = 3\hat{y}$ C/m² for $x<0$, with sheets $\rho_s = 2$ at $x=0$ and $-6$ C/m² at $x=5$ m, and $\mathbf{D}$ uniform in each region (vacuum everywhere). *Tangential:* $\mathbf{E} = 3\hat{y}/\epsilon_0$ is parallel to both planes, tangential $\mathbf{E}$ never jumps, and $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ on both sides, so $D_y = 3$ everywhere (this component is produced by distant charges, not by the sheets). *Normal:* $D_x$ starts at 0, rises by $2$ crossing $x=0$, and by $-6$ crossing $x=5$:
> $$
> \mathbf{D} = \begin{cases}3\hat{y}, & x<0\\ 2\hat{x}+3\hat{y}, & 0<x<5\text{ m}\\ -4\hat{x}+3\hat{y}, & x>5\text{ m}\end{cases}\quad[\text{C/m}^2].
> $$

> [!example] Example — a preview of dielectrics (in-class challenge)
> Medium 1 ($\epsilon_1 = \epsilon_0$, above) meets medium 2 ($\epsilon_2 = 2\epsilon_0$, below) along a plane carrying free charge $\rho_s = 3$ C/m². Below, $\mathbf{D}_2 = 3\hat{x} + 2\hat{y}$ C/m² with $\hat{y}$ the normal. What is $\mathbf{D}_1$? Use $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ in each medium (justified in [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]]).
> - Tangential: $E_{1t} = E_{2t}$ ⇒ $D_{1t}/\epsilon_0 = D_{2t}/(2\epsilon_0) = 3/(2\epsilon_0)$ ⇒ $D_{1t} = \tfrac32$. **Tangential $\mathbf{D}$ is not continuous** — it scales with $\epsilon$.
> - Normal ($\hat{n} = \hat{y}$ from 2 into 1): $D_{1n} - D_{2n} = \rho_s$ ⇒ $D_{1n} = 2 + 3 = 5$.
>
> $\mathbf{D}_1 = \tfrac32\hat{x} + 5\hat{y}$ C/m². The wrong answers on the slide are the standard errors, each one a rule misapplied: $3\hat{x}+2\hat{y}$ (everything continuous), $3\hat{x}+5\hat{y}$ (tangential $\mathbf{D}$ made continuous), $\tfrac32\hat{x} - 1\hat{y}$ (jump subtracted instead of added — an $\hat{n}$ flipped).

## 5. What the conditions do and do not tell you

- They fix **differences** across a surface, never absolute levels. The level comes from elsewhere: a measured field in one region, a symmetry argument, superposition, or a potential at a conductor.
- $\rho_s$ in $\hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{D}_1-\mathbf{D}_2) = \rho_s$ is **free** surface charge. In [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]] the same equation survives inside matter once $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}$; bound charge is accounted for by $\mathbf{P}$.
- At the surface of a conductor in electrostatics the interior field is zero, so the conditions collapse to $\hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_s$ and $\hat{n}\times\mathbf{E} = 0$ with $\hat{n}$ pointing out of the metal: **the field leaves a conductor perpendicularly, and its normal $\mathbf{D}$ *is* the surface charge** ([[1-electrostatics/07-poisson-and-laplace|Lecture 7]] uses this to read plate charges off a field).
- At an interface between two perfect dielectrics there is normally no free charge, so $D_n$ is continuous and $E_t$ is continuous — the pair that makes field lines bend ([[1-electrostatics/09-static-fields-in-dielectric-media|Lecture 9]]).

> [!trap] Four ways to go wrong
> 1. **Mixing up what is continuous.** $E_t$ and $D_n$, not $E_n$ and $D_t$. A quick test: at the surface of a charged conductor, $\mathbf{E}$ is nonzero outside and zero inside — so the *normal* component of $\mathbf{E}$ certainly jumps.
> 2. **Losing $\hat{n}$.** Decide which side is medium 1, point $\hat{n}$ into it, and re-decide at every interface. Swapping both the labels *and* $\hat{n}$ changes nothing; swapping only one flips the sign of $\rho_s$.
> 3. **Applying the differential laws across a boundary.** $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}=\rho$ needs a differentiable $\mathbf{D}$; at a surface charge use the jump condition instead (or a $\delta$-function, if you insist).
> 4. **Units.** (The slides' ink once writes $J_s = I/S$, the volume-current relation — see the [[0-toolkit/04-errata-in-the-course-materials|errata page]].) $\rho_s$ in C/m² and $\mathbf{D}$ in C/m²; $\mathbf{J}_s$ in A/m (current per unit *width*) and $\mathbf{H}$ in A/m. If your jump condition has a stray $\epsilon_0$ in it, you have written $\mathbf{E}$ where $\mathbf{D}$ belongs.

## 6. Summary

- $\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ for static fields: KVL, equivalent to $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$ by Stokes.
- Fields jump only at surface charges and surface currents; the integral laws, squeezed onto the interface, say by how much.
- $\hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{D}_1-\mathbf{D}_2) = \rho_s$, $\hat{n}\times(\mathbf{E}_1-\mathbf{E}_2) = 0$, $\hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{B}_1-\mathbf{B}_2) = 0$, $\hat{n}\times(\mathbf{H}_1-\mathbf{H}_2) = \mathbf{J}_s$, with $\hat{n}$ from 2 into 1.
- Conditions fix jumps, not levels; $\rho_s$ is free charge; $\hat{n}$ is re-chosen at every interface.
