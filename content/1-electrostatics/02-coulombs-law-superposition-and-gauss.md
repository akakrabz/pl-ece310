> [!abstract] In one breath
> A stationary charge $Q$ surrounds itself with a radial field that falls off as $1/r^2$ (Coulomb). Fields of many charges simply **add** (superposition), which lets us integrate over continuous distributions — the "5-step program". Because the field falls off *exactly* as $1/r^2$ while the area of a sphere grows *exactly* as $r^2$, the flux of $\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ through **any** closed surface equals the charge inside: **Gauss's law**, the first Maxwell equation. Lecture 3 turns that into a shortcut.

## 1. Force first, then field

Lecture 1 defined $\mathbf{E}$ operationally: $\mathbf{F}_E = q\mathbf{E}$ on a stationary charge, $\mathbf{F}_M = q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ on a moving one, $\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$ in total. Two quick applications of the magnetic part, because they fix the geometry of $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ in your hands:

> [!example] Velocity selector, then mass selector
> **Crossed fields.** Send a charge with $\mathbf{v}=v_0\hat{x}$ through $\mathbf{E}=E_0\hat{z}$ and $\mathbf{B}=-B_0\hat{y}$. Then $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B} = v_0\hat{x}\times(-B_0\hat{y}) = -v_0B_0\hat{z}$, so
> $$
> \mathbf{F} = q\,(E_0 - v_0B_0)\,\hat{z} = 0 \iff v_0 = E_0/B_0 .
> $$
> Faster particles are bent one way (magnetic force wins), slower ones the other (electric wins); only $v = E/B$ goes straight through the aperture.
>
> **Uniform field, no E.** With $\mathbf{B}=B_0\hat{z}$ and $\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{B}$, the force $q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ has constant magnitude $qv_0B_0$ and is always perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$: uniform circular motion. Equate to the centripetal force, $mv_0^2/R = qv_0B_0$, so $R = mv_0/(qB_0)$. Heavier ions land farther out — a mass spectrometer.

And one identity that will matter for Ampère's law later: a current is charge in motion, so for a wire element $I\,d\mathbf{l} \leftrightarrow q\mathbf{v}$ and the magnetic force on the element is $d\mathbf{F} = I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B}$. (Check the units: A·m = (C/s)·m = C·(m/s). ✓)

## 2. Coulomb's law

Experiments (Coulomb, 1785) on two small charges at rest find a force along the line joining them, proportional to the product of the charges, inversely proportional to the square of the separation, repulsive for like signs.

> [!key] Coulomb's law and the Coulomb field
> Force on $Q_2$ due to $Q_1$ (with $\hat{R}$ the unit vector **from 1 to 2** and $R$ their separation):
> $$
> \mathbf{F}_2 = \frac{Q_1 Q_2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^2}\,\hat{R},\qquad \mathbf{F}_1 = -\mathbf{F}_2 .
> $$
> Divide out the test charge, and the **field of a point charge $Q$ at the origin** is
> $$
> \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\,\hat{r}
> \qquad\text{(units V/m; } \epsilon_0 \approx 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/m)} .
> $$
> Radial, $1/r^2$, pointing *away* from positive charge and *toward* negative charge.

The field is defined as a limit, $\mathbf{E} = \lim_{q\to 0}\mathbf{F}/q$, so that the test charge does not disturb the sources. The course never writes Coulomb's constant as "$k = 9\times10^9$": keep $1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$ explicit, because $\epsilon_0$ is what gets replaced by $\epsilon = \epsilon_0\epsilon_r$ inside materials (introduced with polarization in Lecture 8, used throughout Lecture 9).

> [!trap] Source point vs field point
> Label the **source** 1 and the point where you want the field 2. $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{r}_2-\mathbf{r}_1$ ("final minus initial"), $\hat{R} = \mathbf{R}/|\mathbf{R}|$ points from the source to the field point. If $Q$ is not at the origin,
> $$
> \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\,\frac{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|^{3}},
> $$
> where $\mathbf{r}'$ is the source location. Note the cube: one power of $|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|$ normalizes the direction vector.

### Drawing fields

Two conventions appear on the slides and in every textbook:

- **Arrow plots**: sample the field on a grid and draw an arrow whose length is $|\mathbf{E}|$ (or, more readably, a *unit* vector with the magnitude coded separately).
- **Field lines**: curves everywhere tangent to $\mathbf{E}$. They begin on positive charge and end on negative charge (or run off to infinity); they leave a point charge uniformly in all directions; the *density* of lines is proportional to $|\mathbf{E}|$; and they **never cross**, because $\mathbf{E}$ has exactly one direction at each point — except at *null points* where $\mathbf{E}=0$ and has no direction (the saddle between two like charges). A charge $2Q$ has twice as many lines attached as a charge $Q$ — that is how you tell which charge is bigger in a picture.

## 3. Superposition

Maxwell's equations are linear, and so is Coulomb's law: the field of several charges is the vector sum of their individual fields,

$$
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_n \frac{Q_n}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R_n^2}\,\hat{R}_n,
\qquad \mathbf{R}_n = \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_n .
$$

This is not a small remark; it is the engine of the next three lectures. Here it is in action on the simplest non-trivial configuration.

> [!example] Field on the perpendicular bisector of two opposite charges
> $+Q$ sits at $(0, a/2)$ and $-Q$ at $(0,-a/2)$. Find $\mathbf{E}$ at $P=(D,0)$.
>
> *Symmetry first.* The $x$-components of the two contributions are equal and opposite (one points away from $+Q$, the other toward $-Q$); the $y$-components are equal and both point in $-\hat{y}$. So we only need the $y$-component of one of them and double it: $\mathbf{E} = 2E_{1y}\,\hat{y}$.
>
> *One contribution.* From $+Q$ to $P$: $\mathbf{R} = (D, -a/2)$, $R = \sqrt{D^2 + a^2/4}$, $\hat{R} = (D,-a/2)/R$, so
> $E_{1y} = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^2}\,\hat{R}\cdot\hat{y} = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^2}\left(-\dfrac{a/2}{R}\right)$.
>
> *Result.*
> $$
> \mathbf{E}(D,0) = -\,\frac{Q\,a}{4\pi\epsilon_0\,(D^2 + a^2/4)^{3/2}}\;\hat{y}
> \;\xrightarrow{\;D\gg a\;}\; -\,\frac{Qa}{4\pi\epsilon_0 D^3}\,\hat{y}.
> $$
> Far away, the pair looks like a **dipole** of moment $\mathbf{p} = Qa\,\hat{y}$: on this line (the dipole's *equatorial* or broadside direction) the field is $-\mathbf{p}/(4\pi\epsilon_0D^3)$, antiparallel to $\mathbf{p}$, falling as $1/D^3$ — faster than a point charge's $1/D^2$ — because the two charges nearly cancel. On the dipole's own *axis* (a point $(0,D)$) the two contributions are collinear and *subtract* rather than share a component, and the result is $+2\mathbf{p}/(4\pi\epsilon_0D^3)$: twice as strong and along $\mathbf{p}$. The "double one component" shortcut works only on the bisector.

## 4. Continuous distributions — the 5-step program

Real charge is spread out: on a line ($\rho_l$, C/m), a surface ($\rho_s$, C/m²) or through a volume ($\rho$, C/m³). Superposition becomes an integral over infinitesimal pieces $dQ = \rho_l\,dl = \rho_s\,dS = \rho\,dV$:

$$
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int \frac{dQ'}{R^2}\,\hat{R},
\qquad \hat{R} = \frac{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|},\ R = |\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'| .
$$

> [!recipe] The "patented 5-step program" (use it every time)
> 1. **Make a large, clear drawing.** Cross-sections if 3-D; pick a coordinate system that matches the symmetry.
> 2. **Chop the charge into pieces** $dQ$ and write $dQ$ in terms of the density and the coordinate: $\rho_l\,dz$, $\rho_s\,r\,dr\,d\phi$, …
> 3. **Write $d\mathbf{E}$ of one piece** with Coulomb's law: $d\mathbf{E} = \dfrac{dQ}{4\pi\epsilon_0R^2}\hat{R}$ — and write $\hat{R}$ and $R$ in your coordinates.
> 4. **Use symmetry** to identify components that cancel between mirror-image pieces. Keep only the surviving component (a scalar integral!).
> 5. **Integrate.**
>
> Step 4 is the one students skip, and it is the one that turns a 3-D vector integral into a 1-D scalar one.

> [!trap] Unit vectors are constant only in Cartesian coordinates
> $\hat{x}$ can be pulled out of an integral. $\hat{r}$ and $\hat{\phi}$ (cylindrical, spherical) change direction from point to point and **cannot**. Either resolve them into Cartesian components before integrating, or use symmetry so that the surviving component is along a *fixed* direction at the field point. See [[0-toolkit/01-coordinates-and-differential-elements]].

1. *Drawing / coordinates.* Cylindrical $(r,\phi,z)$; the field point is $(r, \phi, 0)$.
2. *Pieces.* $dQ = \rho_l\,dz$ at height $z$.
3. *One piece.* $R = \sqrt{r^2+z^2}$; $d\mathbf{E} = \dfrac{\rho_l\,dz}{4\pi\epsilon_0(r^2+z^2)}\hat{R}$, where $\hat{R}$ points from the element to $P$, making angle $\alpha$ with the horizontal, $\cos\alpha = r/R$.
4. *Symmetry.* The element at $-z$ produces the mirror image of $d\mathbf{E}$: $z$-components cancel, radial components add. Only $dE_r = dE\cos\alpha$ survives, and $\hat{r}$ at $P$ is a *fixed* direction (it depends on $\phi$ of $P$, not on $z$ of the source), so it can come outside.
5. *Integrate.*
   $$
   E_r = \frac{\rho_l}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{-a}^{a}\frac{r\,dz}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}} .
   $$
   Substitute $z = r\tan\alpha$, $dz = r\sec^2\alpha\,d\alpha$, $r^2+z^2 = r^2\sec^2\alpha$:
   $$
   E_r = \frac{\rho_l}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}\int_{-\alpha_0}^{\alpha_0}\cos\alpha\,d\alpha
       = \frac{\rho_l}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}\,2\sin\alpha_0
       = \frac{\rho_l}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}\,\frac{2a}{\sqrt{a^2+r^2}} .
   $$

> [!key] Infinite line charge
> Let $a\to\infty$: $\dfrac{2a}{\sqrt{a^2+r^2}}\to 2$, so
> $$
> \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho_l}{2\pi\epsilon_0\, r}\,\hat{r}
> $$
> The field of a line falls off as $1/r$ — **not** $1/r^2$ like a point charge — because charge farther along the line keeps contributing as you move away. (Lecture 3 gets this in two lines with Gauss's law; the "same result" cross-check is a favourite of the course.)

The two "review at home" examples in the slides follow the identical pattern: a **ring** of radius $a$ with line charge $\rho_{l0}$ ($dQ = \rho_{l0}\,a\,d\phi$, total $2\pi a\rho_{l0}$), and the force of a **charged rod** — total charge $Q$ spread over length $L$ along the $x$-axis — on a point charge $q$ sitting on that axis a distance $a$ beyond the rod's end: $\mathbf{F} = \dfrac{qQ}{4\pi\epsilon_0\,a(L+a)}\hat{x}$, where $\hat{x}$ comes out of the integral precisely because it is Cartesian.

## 5. From Coulomb to Gauss

Now the idea that makes Lecture 3 possible. Surround a point charge $Q$ by a sphere of radius $r$ and compute the **flux** of $\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ through it (the flux integral $\oint_S\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ adds up the normal component; Lecture 3 develops the intuition):

$$
\oint_S \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{S}
= \epsilon_0\,\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\;\underbrace{(4\pi r^2)}_{\text{sphere area}} = Q .
$$

On the sphere, $\mathbf{E}$ is constant in magnitude and parallel to the outward $d\mathbf{S}$, so the integral is just $|\mathbf{E}|\times$area — and the $r^2$ of the area cancels the $1/r^2$ of Coulomb's law **exactly**. The result does not depend on $r$.

Three observations turn this into a law:

- **Any surface.** Field lines that leave $Q$ never stop; each one crosses any enclosing surface exactly once net (a wiggly surface may be crossed three times, but two of those cancel). So the flux is $Q$ through *any* closed surface around $Q$, not only a sphere — and it is **zero** for a closed surface that does *not* enclose $Q$ (every line that enters also leaves).
- **Superposition.** Flux is linear in $\mathbf{E}$, so with many charges the fluxes add: $Q_1 + Q_2 + \dots$ for the charges inside, nothing for those outside.
- **Naming.** Define the **electric flux density** $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ (units C/m²) so that flux comes out in coulombs. (In materials, Lecture 8, $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}$, and it is $\mathbf{D}$ that Gauss's law keeps counting free charge with.)

> [!key] Gauss's law — the first Maxwell equation
> $$
> \oint_S \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{\text{enc}} = \int_V \rho\,dV
> $$
> for **any** closed surface $S$ bounding volume $V$, with $d\mathbf{S}$ **outward**. Flux out = charge enclosed. Charges outside $S$ contribute nothing to the *flux* (they do contribute to $\mathbf{E}$ on $S$; their contributions to the flux just cancel).

Gauss's law is *derived* here from Coulomb's law, but it is more fundamental: it stays true for moving charges and time-varying fields, where Coulomb's law fails. As the notes put it, "at this stage the introduction of Gauss's law may seem unnecessary"; Lecture 3 shows what it buys us — the line-charge result above in two lines instead of a substitution integral — and Lecture 4 shows that it is one of the four equations that govern everything.

## 6. Summary

| Configuration | $\mathbf{E}$ | falls off as |
|---|---|---|
| point charge $Q$ | $\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\hat{r}$ | $1/r^2$ |
| dipole $p=Qa$, far away | $\dfrac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 D^3}$ on the bisector (antiparallel to $\mathbf{p}$); $\dfrac{2p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 D^3}$ on the axis (along $\mathbf{p}$) | $1/D^3$ |
| infinite line $\rho_l$ | $\dfrac{\rho_l}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r}\hat{r}$ | $1/r$ |
