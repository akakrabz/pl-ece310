(c) What is the smallest number of zeros $L \ge 0$ that accomplishes the goal of part (b)?

(d) Pad with the smallest $L$ of part (c), so that $Y[k]$ is a DFT of length $M = {{params.N}} + L$. {{#params.dig}}Find the index $k$ with $Y[k] = X_d\left({{params.wb_tex}}\right)$ and the index $k'$, $0 \le k' \le M - 1$, with $Y[k'] = X_d^*\left({{params.wb_tex}}\right)$.{{/params.dig}} {{#params.hz}}Find the index $k$, $0 \le k \le M - 1$, at which $Y[k]$ evaluates the spectrum at $+{{params.Fb}}$ Hz, and the index $k'$, $0 \le k' \le M - 1$, at which it evaluates the spectrum at $-{{params.Fb}}$ Hz.{{/params.hz}}

For a signal of length at most $N$, $X[k] = X_d\left(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right)$: the bins are $\frac{2\pi}{N}$ apart. $X_d$ is $2\pi$-periodic, and for a real $x$, $X_d(-\omega) = X_d^*(\omega)$. Zero padding to length $M$ does not change $X_d$; it evaluates it at $\frac{2\pi k}{M}$ instead.{{#params.hz}} An analog frequency $f$ in Hz is the digital frequency $\omega = \frac{2\pi f}{f_s}$.{{/params.hz}} $\omega$ is a bin of the length-$M$ DFT when $k = M\cdot\frac{\omega}{2\pi}$ is an integer. Work with $\frac{\omega}{2\pi}$, not $\frac{\omega}{\pi}$, and test both frequencies: $M$ must be a multiple of both denominators. It is the total length $M$ that matters, not the number $L$ of zeros.

(a) $X[k] = X_d(\omega_k)$ with $\omega_k = \frac{2\pi k}{ {{params.N}} } = {{params.dw_tex}}\,k$; $X_d$ is $2\pi$-periodic, and $x$ is real, so $X_d(-\omega) = X_d^*(\omega)$.

Here $\frac{\omega_1}{2\pi} = {{params.f1_tex}}$ and $\frac{\omega_2}{2\pi} = {{params.f2_tex}}$,{{/params.dig}} {{#params.hz}}The bins are $\frac{f_s}{M}$ Hz apart, so $f$ sits on bin $k = \frac{M f}{f_s}$ when that is an integer ($\omega = \frac{2\pi f}{f_s}$, $k = M\frac{\omega}{2\pi}$). Here $\frac{f_1}{f_s} = \frac{ {{params.F1}} }{ {{params.fs}} } = {{params.f1_tex}}$ and $\frac{f_2}{f_s} = \frac{ {{params.F2}} }{ {{params.fs}} } = {{params.f2_tex}}$,{{/params.hz}} so $M$ must be a multiple of ${{params.q1}}$ and of ${{params.q2}}$, i.e. of $\operatorname{lcm}({{params.q1}}, {{params.q2}}) = {{params.q}}$:
